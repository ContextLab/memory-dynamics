\begin{abstract}\noindent
What is a second telling of an experience a telling of?
Memories change with time and with retrieval, so each recounting may be shaped less by the experience itself than by how we last recounted it.
Here we use a geometric framework to compare two recountings of the same experience.
We model an experience and each recounting of it as trajectories through a semantic embedding space, then segment each trajectory into events.
We applied our approach to data collected as participants watched a television episode, verbally recounted it immediately, and recounted it again one week later.
Participants' delayed recountings resembled their own immediate recountings more closely than they resembled the episode itself.
Their structure was specific enough to identify the immediate recounting that preceded them.
Delayed recountings were also well described as geometrically simplified versions of immediate recountings, in that the events whose removal least distorts a trajectory's shape were those most likely to be dropped.
What we retrieve after a delay therefore appears to be a person-specific distillation, rather than the experience as it was first encountered.
\end{abstract}


\section*{Introduction}

Suppose that you attend a friend's wedding.
That evening you describe it to your partner, recounting how the ceremony ran long, how the best man's toast was unexpectedly moving, and how a small child wandered onto the dance floor midway through the first song.
A week later a coworker asks how the wedding was, and you tell the story again.
What is that second telling a telling of?
One appealing answer is that both tellings draw on the same underlying memory of the wedding, and that whatever differences arise between them reflect the ordinary imprecision of putting an experience into words.
A second possibility, which motivates the work we report here, is that your first telling has itself become part of what you are remembering.

What happens to an experience between living it and describing it later?
Since our brains do not maintain an exhaustive account of everything we encounter, the very act of perceiving an experience already necessarily entails compressing and distorting it.
We attend closely to some aspects of a scene while ignoring others, and we tend to interpret ambiguous moments in light of what has happened previously and what we expect will happen next.
But once an experience has ended, neither the experience itself nor our moment-to-moment perception of it remains available, to us or to anyone else.
What remains is instead the product of a further distillation, through which our perception of the experience is reduced to something our memory systems can store and later reconstruct.
Which aspects of an experience survive this second distillation may depend on what we attended to, what we anticipated would matter later, what we found interesting, and the thoughts, motivations, and biases we brought with us.
Because remembering entails reconstructing an experience rather than replaying it, the resulting account often preserves the general ``shape'' of what happened while discarding many of its particulars~\citep{Bart32,SchaEtal11,Scha12}.
The units over which these distillations operate appear to be events, in that people segment continuous experience into discrete episodes at moments of change, and those boundaries shape what is remembered afterwards~\citep{RadvZack17,BaldEtal17}.
In prior work we characterized this transformation geometrically by modeling an experience and a person's recounting of it as trajectories through a semantic space, and asking which properties of the experience's trajectory are preserved in the recounting~\citep{HeusEtal21}.

Yet memories do not remain fixed once they have been encoded, pointing to further transformations that a single recounting cannot reveal.
Information may be lost over time; experiences we have after an event may color how we later remember it; similar experiences may interfere with one another; and we may simply re-evaluate what happened in light of what we have learned since.
The act of retrieving a memory itself appears to be one of the forces that changes it, in that a memory can become labile when it is reactivated and must be stabilized again afterwards~\citep{NadeEtal00,HupbEtal07,HupbEtal09,SedeEtal11}.
Retrieval also changes what will be accessible later---often substantially---relative to memories that go unretrieved~\citep{RoedKarp06a,KarpRoed07}.
Further, the particular way in which we recount an experience appears to matter as well, since people who are asked to describe an event for one purpose later remember it in ways that are biased toward that description~\citep{TverMars00}.
Memories also become more semantic and less perceptually detailed, both with the passage of time and with repeated remembering~\citep{LifaEtal21}.
Repeated retrieval of naturalistic events over the course of a week has been found to stabilize each person's recall (for example, which details they include) without causing different people's recalls to converge~\citep{MeleEtal25}.
Taken together, these observations suggest that each time we remember an experience, the substance of the underlying memory we are retrieving may differ from what we retrieved before.

How might we compare two recountings of the same experience when the experience and both recountings take the form of unconstrained natural language?
Recent work has begun to use language models to quantify the content of what individuals say when they recount what they remember~\citep{HeusEtal21,MannEtal22,FitzEtal26,FeneEtal25}.
The approach developed in our prior work embeds the description of an experience, and each individual's recounting of it, into a common high-dimensional space whose coordinates reflect the concepts under consideration, and through which the experience and recountings each unfold over time as a geometric trajectory~\citep{HeusEtal21,FitzEtal26,VeraEtal25}.
We then segment the experience and each recounting into events and identify which event from the experience each recall event describes, yielding sequences of events that may be compared directly across people and across delays~\citep{ChenEtal17,BaldEtal17,HeusEtal21}.

Here we apply our approach to data from an experiment in which participants viewed an episode of a television show, recounted its events immediately afterward, and then recounted them again following a week-long delay.
Because we collected two recountings of the same experience from each participant, we are able to ask what participants' delayed recountings most closely resemble.
If retrieval recovered the original experience in an uncorrupted form, then both recountings should be about equally similar to the experience itself.
We find instead that the structure of a participant's delayed recounting is more similar to that of their own immediate recounting than to that of the original episode.
It is also more similar to their own immediate recounting than to other participants' immediate recountings, to the degree that an individual's immediate recall can be identified from their delayed recall on the basis of this structure alone.
What governs retrieval after a delay therefore appears to be a person-specific distillation, rather than the episode as it was first experienced.

Capturing the semantic geometry of individuals' recalls at different points in time also offers the opportunity to characterize the transformations undergone by our memories beyond an initial recounting.
For example, if individuals' recountings tend to retain their idiosyncratic structures after a delay, how \textit{do} the structures of our memories tend to change with time?
There is a particular qualitative change our memories undergo as we naturally forget aspects of experience, implied by common descriptors such as ``fading'', ``blurring'', becoming ``fuzzy'', or ``decaying''~\citep{Thor13}.
But the nature of this change can be difficult to characterize more concretely.
Here we show that this transformation is well described as geometrically simplifying the path through semantic space an individual follows as they recount an experience, in that applying an existing method for removing detail from a geometric curve to participants' immediate recalls preferentially removes events those individuals will forget following the delay.


\section*{Results}

We ran an experiment in which we asked participants to view and verbally recount episodes of television shows across two experiment sessions, separated by approximately one week (Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm}).
In the first session, participants viewed the pilot episode of the TV series \textit{Atlanta}.
They were then given up to one hour (and a minimum of 10 minutes) to recount what had happened in the episode to the best of their abilities (i.e., immediate recall).
After they finished recounting the episode, we asked participants to verbally predict what they thought would happen in the show's next episode.
Participants then returned for a second session between 6 and 8 days later, in which they began by again verbally recounting the \textit{Atlanta} episode they had viewed the week prior (i.e., delayed recall).
Following the delayed recall phase, participants viewed and recounted either the second episode of \textit{Atlanta} or the pilot episode of a different TV series, \textit{Arrested Development}.
We designed our experiment to support exploring of a range of behavioral memory phenomena, including differences between immediate and delayed recall, influences of contextually congruent versus incongruent prior experience, effects of self-cuing via context reinstatement, and relationships between
successful prediction and subsequent memory.
In the present study, we focus on characterizing how the content and structure of participants' recountings of the first \textit{Atlanta} episode changed between their immediate and delayed recalls.

To characterize how participants preserved and distorted the content of the \textit{Atlanta} episode in their immediate and delayed recountings, we adapted an approach we developed in prior work that casts experiences and memories as trajectories unfolding in a common high-dimensional representation space~\citep{HeusEtal21}.
We construct these trajectories using a text embedding model.
Briefly, text embedding models take in natural language text of an arbitrary length and output a fixed-length vector representation that captures the text's semantic meaning.
Intuitively, an output vector of length $n$ can be viewed as a coordinate in an $n$-dimensional text embedding space, wherein texts with similar semantic meanings are assigned to nearby coordinates while those with divergent meanings reside farther apart.
In this way, the distance between the embedding coordinates assigned to two texts provides a measure of how semantically similar their underlying contents are.

We began by creating detailed text annotations describing the content present during each individual camera shot from the episode (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Text}; see \nameref{subsec:annotations-transcripts}).
We then projected each camera shot's text description onto a coordinate in a 768-dimensional text embedding space using EmbeddingGemma~\citep{VeraEtal25}.
The sequence of embedding space coordinates across successive camera shots thus forms a trajectory that characterizes how the \textit{Atlanta} episode's latent semantic content unfolded over time (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Trajectories}).
Since the durations of camera shots varied over the course of the episode, we resampled the episode's trajectory to a constant temporal resolution of one vector per second.

Next, we transcribed each participant's immediate and delayed verbal recounting of the episode content (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Text}).
In constructing trajectories from participants' recountings, we reasoned that casual, unrehearsed speech tends to be information-sparse compared to written text (e.g., it might take multiple spoken sentences to describe the same content or occurrence that could be more concisely described in a single written sentence).
We therefore parsed each participant's recall transcript from each session into overlapping sliding windows spanning (up to) 5 consecutive sentences each, and transformed the text comprising each of these sliding windows into a text embedding vector.
This resulted in a trajectory characterizing the dynamic semantic content of each participant's immediate and delayed recounting (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Trajectories}).

Neither the episode's trajectory nor participants' recall trajectories moved through the embedding space at a constant rate.
Both tended to linger within a region for a period before shifting relatively abruptly to another, and we treated these periods of relative stability as \textit{events} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Event-segmentation}; see \nameref{subsec:eventseg}).
Given this observation, we used a hidden Markov model (HMM)~\citep{BaumPetr66,BaldEtal17,KumaEtal21} to segment the episode's trajectory, selecting the number of events by finding the segmentation whose within-event timepoint correlations were most distinct from its across-event correlations, as quantified by the Wasserstein distance between the two distributions (computed over pairs of timepoints separated by no more than the longest event, so that the two distributions are drawn from comparable temporal lags).
This yielded 20 events, each summarized (as a single 768-dimensional coordinate) by averaging the embedding vectors across the timepoints it spanned (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Events}).

Whereas the episode's events had to be discovered, segmenting participants' recountings called for a different approach: rather than finding a separate set of events within each recall (as we had for the episode), we sought to determine which of the episode's events a participant was describing at each moment.
Specifically, we treated the episode's 20 events as a fixed set of states, scored each recall window by its similarity to each of them, and decoded the highest-scoring sequence of episode events being described~\citep{Vite67}.
Because participants may recount events out of order, and may return to an event they have already described (e.g., to add a detail they had left out the first time), we permitted transitions between any pair of states, including re-entry into a state already visited.
Transitions were penalized according to their type: no penalty for remaining within an event, a modest penalty for advancing to the next, and a larger penalty for any other jump (see \nameref{subsec:eventseg}).
Collapsing runs of consecutive windows assigned to the same episode event yields, for each recounting, a sequence of recall events together with a label indicating which episode event each one describes (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \textit{Events}).

Together, these steps yielded three descriptions of the same episode: the sequence of events comprising the episode itself, each participant's sequences of immediate and delayed recall events, and a mapping from every recall event onto the episode event it describes.
Because all three are expressed in a common set of units, they may be compared directly, both across participants and across the delay.

The most basic of these comparisons concerns how much of the episode each participant recounted (i.e., how many of its 20 events they described at least once).
Immediate recountings described 16.98 of the episode's 20 events on average (s.d.~2.22), whereas delayed recountings described 16.26 (s.d.~2.51), a mean decline of 0.72 events (Wilcoxon signed-rank $T = 581.0,~p = 0.003,~r = 0.49,~95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.23, 1.19]$ events, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{event-recall-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [7]}]}).
% Cells are identified by the In[] label they carry in the committed notebook,
% i.e. the execution count of the run whose output is stored there.
Participants covered slightly less of the episode after a week than they had immediately after viewing it.
A count of this kind establishes how much was left out, but says nothing about \textit{what} was left out.
The same decline in the number of events recounted would follow whether every participant shed whichever events were easiest to shed, or whether each participant shed a different handful.

We next asked whether the events participants left out were an arbitrary subset.
For each of the episode's 20 events, we computed the proportion of participants who described that event at least once (counting a participant who returned to the same event more than once within a session only once).
Had every event been equally likely to be recounted, these proportions would be expected to differ only by chance.
Instead, we found that the proportion of participants describing a given event ranged from 0.64 to 0.96 in immediate recall (Cochran's $Q(19) = 75.07,~p = 1.3 \times 10^{-8}$, Kendall's $W = 0.075$) and from 0.58 to 0.98 after the delay ($Q(19) = 73.82,~p = 2.1 \times 10^{-8}$, $W = 0.073$; $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{event-recall-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [8]}]}).
In other words, some of the episode's events were consistently more likely to be recounted than others, although the size of this effect was modest.

This profile also changed little across the delay: the proportion of participants describing a given event immediately was strongly related to the proportion describing it a week later (Spearman's $\rho = 0.795,~p = 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$, $n = 20$ events; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{event-recall-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [10]}]}).
The events most participants omitted after the delay were largely the ones they had omitted to begin with.
Because this relationship is symmetric, it shows that the two profiles agree, and not that the earlier one produced the later one.
We note that these are statements about events, averaged over participants, and that they say nothing about whether any of this structure is specific to a given individual.
For example, two participants could produce identical profiles while recounting the same events in quite different orders, and in quite different terms.

Given that participants' omissions were systematic across the group, we wondered whether the recountings themselves carried any person-specific signature, beginning with the order in which participants described the episode's events.
For every pair of episode events described in both of a participant's recountings, we asked whether the two were described in the same relative order (a graded quantity, since a participant may describe the same event more than once), and summarized the agreement between sessions as a single measure of order similarity.
Establishing that a participant's two recountings agree is not by itself informative, since the episode imposes an order that every participant partly follows; the question is whether they agree more closely than that shared constraint already implies.
We therefore compared each participant's delayed recounting to their own immediate recounting and to every other participant's immediate recounting, ranked the former within the distribution of the latter (i.e., computed a percentile rank), and tested those ranks against chance (a logic we apply to every comparison we report below).
Participants' delayed recountings did resemble their own immediate recountings in order more than they resembled other participants' (Wilcoxon signed-rank $T = 1076.5,~p = 7.0 \times 10^{-4},~r = 0.51$, median percentile rank 0.76, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.60, 0.81]$, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{order-analysis-and-fig.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [9]}]}; Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-order}A--D).
This group-level agreement did not, however, extend to identifying individual participants: a participant's own immediate recounting was the closest of all 53 in order for 5.2\% of participants once ties were broken at random, which is not reliably above the 1.9\% expected by chance (one-sided binomial $p = 0.08$, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{identification-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [8]}]}; Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-order}E).
Ties were common enough at the top of these rankings that only 2 of the 53 participants had their own immediate recounting as an outright best match.

Order, however, provides only a coarse description of a recounting, in that it treats events as interchangeable labels and discards everything about how their contents relate to one another (e.g., whether two events were described in similar terms or in quite different ones).
An important feature of our approach is that it places the episode and both recountings in a common embedding space, which allows us to ask a stronger question: whether the \textit{structure} of a participant's path through the episode's content is preserved, and not merely the sequence in which they visited it.
To assess this, we compared event sequences using dynamic time warping (DTW), which aligns two sequences of differing length by finding the order-preserving correspondence between their events that minimizes the total distance between matched pairs~\citep{Vint68,SakoChib78}.
The average distance (here, cosine distance) across matched pairs provides a measure of how similar the two sequences' structures are.

If retrieval after a delay recovered the episode as it was originally experienced, a participant's delayed recounting should resemble the episode at least as closely as it resembles their own earlier recounting of it.
Instead, we found that delayed recountings aligned more closely with the participant's own immediate recounting than with the episode itself (mean alignment costs 0.53, s.d.~0.10, and 0.65, s.d.~0.06, respectively; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $T_w = 1.0,~p = 2.5 \times 10^{-10},~r = 1.00$, mean difference 0.12, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.10, 0.14]$, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{dtw-analyses-and-fig.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [11]}]}; Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-structure}A,C,E).
We next asked whether this resemblance was specific to the participant who produced it, and found that each participant's delayed recounting was structurally closer to their own immediate recounting than to those of other participants ($T = 1431.0,~p = 2.2 \times 10^{-11},~r = 1.00$, median percentile rank 1.00, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{dtw-analyses-and-fig.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [12]}]}; Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-structure}A,B,D).
Unlike recall order, this effect was strong enough to identify individual participants: for 36 of the 53 participants, the delayed recounting aligned more closely with their own immediate recounting than with any of the other 52 (68\%, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [55, 79]\%$, against 1.9\% expected by chance; one-sided binomial $p = 2.0 \times 10^{-49}$), and for 91\% it ranked among the five closest (\textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{identification-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [10]}]}; Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-structure}F,G).
In other words, what a participant retrieved after a week reflected something specific to how they had described the episode before.

Establishing that this structure is preserved is not the same as establishing that it is unchanged.
Every measure to this point is a comparison, in that it reports whether two things are more or less alike, and none of them describes what the change between them consists of.
To characterize the transformation itself, we hypothesized that if a delayed recounting is a simplified version of an earlier one, removing detail from the earlier one should bring the two closer together.
We used the Ramer-Douglas-Peucker algorithm~\citep{Rame72,DougPeuc73} (a standard method for simplifying polylines) to simplify each participant's immediate recall trajectory, discarding events that lay close to the line between their neighbors while retaining those whose removal would change the path's shape.
We swept the algorithm's tolerance ($\epsilon$, expressed as a cosine distance) across its full range in steps of 0.01, selecting for each participant the value that best aligned their simplified immediate trajectory with their delayed one.
We note that this simplification was performed on the full 768-dimensional trajectories, and that the two-dimensional projections in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} serve only to visualize the result.

Participants who left out more events across the delay were the participants for whom more aggressive simplification (i.e., a larger $\epsilon$) fit best.
The number of events removed at the best-fitting tolerance tracked the decline in the number of events a participant recounted (Kendall's $\tau = 0.518,~p = 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.36, 0.66]$, $n = 53$), as did the tolerance itself ($\tau = 0.457,~p = 1.9 \times 10^{-5}$, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.29, 0.61]$, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{rdp-analyses.ipynb}, cells \texttt{In [13]} and \texttt{In [14]}]}).
Simplification also predicted \textit{which} events would go: the events it removed from a participant's immediate recall trajectory coincided with the events that participant stopped recounting more often than chance would predict (22 events identified correctly in total, against 12.9 expected; $p = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}$, Monte Carlo test over 10,000 hypergeometric draws, $n = 53$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{rdp-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [17]}]}).
Because simplification can never remove the first or last event of a trajectory, both were excluded from this comparison.
Further, this prediction was person-specific, in that simplifying a participant's own trajectory predicted their forgetting better than simplifying other participants' trajectories did (Wilcoxon signed-rank $T = 272.0,~p = 0.007,~r = 0.55$, median percentile rank 0.71, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.52, 0.84]$, $n = 26$; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{rdp-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [18]}]}).
The same logic applies one step earlier in the chain, to the episode's own trajectory: simplifying the episode at the tolerance that best aligned it to a given participant's immediate recounting removed events that participant had left out of that recounting more often than chance would predict (22 events identified correctly in total, against 12.1 expected; $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$, the smallest value 10,000 hypergeometric draws can resolve, $n = 53$; \textbf{[SUPPLEMENTARY FIGURE]}; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{episode-simplification-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [7]}]}).
This last result rests on a minority of the sample, in that no simplification of the episode aligned better than the full episode trajectory did for 38 of the 53 participants, leaving 15 who make any prediction at all (among those 15, mean excess over chance 0.66 events, $95\%~\mathrm{CI} = [0.26, 1.03]$).
The corresponding statement about the episode as a whole, that the events simplification removes are the ones fewest participants described, does not hold (Spearman's $\rho = -0.32,~p = 0.20$, $n = 18$ events; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{episode-simplification-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [9]}]}).

Three features of this analysis qualify what it can show.
First, the comparison against other participants' trajectories could be made only for the 26 participants (of 53) who both stopped recounting at least one event and were predicted to stop recounting at least one; because that subset is defined partly by the outcome being predicted, it is not a neutral sample.
Second, for 27 of the 53 participants the tolerance that best aligned the two recountings was zero, meaning that no simplification improved the alignment (median $\epsilon = 0.00$, range 0.00--0.82; median 0 events removed, mean 1.00; \textbf{[CHECK THIS: \texttt{rdp-analyses.ipynb}, cell \texttt{In [11]}]}), and ties were resolved in favor of the smallest tolerance, so some of those zeros record the absence of an improvement rather than a positive fit at $\epsilon = 0$.
Third, $\epsilon$ is selected for each participant by aligning against the same delayed recounting whose omissions are then predicted, so the fit and the test draw on the same data; the comparison against other participants' trajectories is what guards against this.
A further limit follows from the resolution at which we measured rather than from the memories themselves.
Segmenting a trajectory into events is itself a form of simplification, in that it replaces a continuous path with a small number of representative coordinates (one per event) and discards whatever varied within an event.
Any pruning a participant performed below that scale is invisible to us, absorbed into our own rounding before the trajectory is simplified further.
Overall, the relationships reported above describe a tendency across the sample at a fixed common resolution, and they do not imply that every delayed recounting is a visibly pruned copy of its predecessor.

\section*{Discussion}

We asked participants to watch an episode of a television show, to recount what they remembered immediately afterwards, and to recount the episode again following a week-long delay.
Embedding the episode's annotations and each recounting into a common semantic space allowed us to represent all three as trajectories, to segment each trajectory into events, and to compare them on common terms.
Participants recalled the episode's events in a similar order before and after the delay.
The semantic structure of a participant's delayed recall was more similar to the structure of their own immediate recall than to that of the episode, and it was more similar to their own immediate recall than to other participants' immediate recalls.
Delayed recalls were also well described as simplified versions of immediate recalls, in that removing detail from a participant's immediate recall trajectory brought it closer to the delayed recall trajectory that followed it.

Taken together, these results suggest a way of thinking about what recounting an experience does to it.
If experiences and memories may be described as trajectories through a semantic space, then a recounting is a traversal of one of those trajectories~\citep{HeusEtal21}.
Semantic space differs from physical space in that nothing prevents a narrator from jumping between distant locations, and yet participants' recountings turn out to be strongly autocorrelated in practice, which is the regularity that our event-segmentation approach relies on.
Because movement through the space is relatively continuous, describing how one arrives at a later event from an earlier one implies that the intervening region was traversed in some sense, whether it was articulated explicitly or left for the listener to fill in.
Some events therefore behave like control points, in that removing one substantively changes the shape of the path.
Others behave like waypoints, which fall close to the line between their neighbors and may be removed with little consequence, since travelling between the surrounding control points already implies passing near them.
Trajectory simplification provides a way of telling the two apart, and this distinction appears to hold at both of the transitions we can examine.
The events that simplification removes from the episode's own trajectory are events the participant it was simplified to fit had left out of their immediate recounting, and the events that simplification removes from a participant's immediate recall trajectory are the ones that participant tended to drop across the delay.

That not all events are equally memorable is well established.
Gist and central details, for example, survive delays that perceptual and peripheral details do not~\citep{HeusEtal21,SekeEtal16,SekeEtal18,GilbMosc21}, and autobiographical narratives shed some classes of content faster than others~\citep{DiamLevi20,SuEtal26}.
Memories also become more semantic and less perceptually detailed with the passage of time and with repeated remembering~\citep{LifaEtal21}, and events with stronger connections to the events around them are better remembered than more isolated ones~\citep{LeeChen22,AntoEtal24}.
What our results add is a change in where that asymmetry comes from.
Centrality is ordinarily established from the outside, by asking raters which details matter to the plot, or by sorting content into categories fixed in advance.
Here it follows from the geometry of an individual's own trajectory, and it is computable from that trajectory alone.
An event is central because of where it sits relative to the rest of the path, and not because a rater judged it to be important.
This turns the observation that some events are more memorable than others into a candidate explanation of why.

Why should waypoints be the events that go?
One possibility is that they are redundant, in the sense that a listener, or the rememberer's own future self, can recover them from the events on either side.
Another is that they are simply weaker in themselves, for example less distinctive, less emotionally charged, or less causally connected to the rest of the narrative~\citep{AntoEtal24}.
These accounts make similar predictions about which events will be forgotten, but they differ in what those predictions should depend on.
If events are dropped because they are intrinsically weak, then a simplification derived from any participant's trajectory should predict forgetting about as well as one derived from the participant's own, since weak events would be weak for everyone.
If they are dropped because they are recoverable from their neighbors, then a participant's own trajectory should do better, since control-point status is defined relative to the rest of that person's path.
Our results favor the latter account.
Read this way, dropping a waypoint is an economy rather than a loss, and it is worth noting that events which go unreported are not always unavailable, since a shift in perspective at retrieval can recover details that an earlier recounting left out~\citep{AndePich78,OedeEtal17}.

The same argument implies that what counts as a control point should be idiosyncratic.
An event's status depends on the path it sits within, and different people who watched the same episode construct different paths through it, so the events whose removal would deform one person's trajectory need not be the events that matter for another's.
This is what we take the specificity of the delayed recalls to mean.
That a delayed recall can be matched to the immediate recall which preceded it, on the basis of structure alone, is a fingerprint in the sense that the term is used for the neural signatures by which individuals may be identified from functional connectivity or from their responses to naturalistic stimuli~\citep{FinnEtal15,FinnEtal20,AndeEtal20}.
The parallel lies in the identification logic rather than in the measurement, since ours is a behavioral signature, recovered from what a participant said rather than from how their brain responded.
People differ, for example, in where they place event boundaries in a continuous experience~\citep{SavaEtal23}, in how much of their recall structure they share with others~\citep{ChenEtal17,NguyEtal19}, and in the character of their autobiographical remembering more generally~\citep{PaloEtal18}.
Repeated retrieval of naturalistic events has been found to stabilize each person's recall without causing different people's recalls to converge~\citep{MeleEtal25}, which is what one would expect if each person is stabilizing their own set of control points.

Everything we measured came from a verbal recounting, and so a reasonable objection is that control points and waypoints are properties of storytelling rather than of memory.
We think that dichotomy is less clean than it first appears.
Recounting is the only observable available in a study of this kind, so the two are confounded by construction, and no analysis of transcripts alone can fully separate them.
More to the point, the way a person tells a story is not merely a window onto what they remember, but also an input to what they will remember next.
Retelling an event for a particular audience or purpose, for example, biases how it is recalled later~\citep{TverMars00,Mars07}, and reproducing a narrative repeatedly reshapes it in ways that persist~\citep{BergRoed99,StJaScha13}.
If the function of recounting is to let a listener, or one's own future self, re-traverse the trajectory of the original experience, then communicative efficiency and mnemonic efficiency are not competing explanations so much as the same pressure seen from two sides.

We acknowledge several limitations in the current study.
First, every participant recalled the episode immediately, and so we cannot separate two accounts of what we observed.
A delayed recall may resemble the immediate recall because the immediate recall was itself re-encoded and then retrieved a week later, or because a single stable memory was sampled twice and each sampling was shaped by the same person-specific distillation.
Our measures are symmetric and cannot establish direction, and the design lacks an arm in which the immediate recall is withheld.
Designs that do include such a control, in which retrieved information is later found nearer to what was retrieved than to what was originally presented, offer the strongest evidence that retrieval writes its own output back into the trace~\citep{BridPall12,NadeEtal00,NadeHard09}.
Second, we used one episode, one delay, and one intervening recounting, and it remains to be seen how the picture changes across stimuli, across longer intervals, or across many successive retellings~\citep{ChanEtal09b,LifaEtal21}.
Third, control-point status is defined with respect to one particular simplification algorithm, and a different algorithm might well partition the same events differently.
Fourth, our procedure matches every recall event to an event in the episode, so content that a participant introduced but that does not appear in the episode falls outside what we are able to measure.
Fifth, our measure of what connects one event to another is built from semantic similarity, and so it cannot on its own distinguish events that are close in meaning from events that are causally linked~\citep{LeeChen22,AntoEtal24,XuEtal24b}.
Sixth, segmenting a trajectory into events is itself a form of simplification, performed by our analysis rather than by the participant, and it sets the resolution at which everything else is measured.
Replacing a continuous path with a sequence of event-average coordinates discards whatever varied within an event.
Any detail a participant lost below that scale is therefore absorbed into our own rounding before we look for it.
Our choice of how finely to segment, together with the length of the episode and of the recall period, therefore places a floor on the forgetting we are able to detect.
A participant whose recounting appears unchanged may simply have changed it in ways too fine for us to resolve.
We take some reassurance from the fact that the same segmentation is fit and applied to the episode and to both recountings alike.
It therefore provides a common basis for comparison, rather than a transformation applied selectively to the sequences being compared.

Speculatively, one way to think about how this might work is in terms of attractor dynamics~\citep{Hopf82}.
Rather than supposing that we hold literal duplicate copies of a memory, we might imagine that laying down a memory carves out a basin in an energy landscape, and that remembering the experience deepens a basin near wherever we happen to land.
Combined with the observation that remembering distorts, this implies that what gets re-encoded on each occasion may not be the original memory, but a distorted version of it.
Successive retrievals might then arrive at a basin corresponding to the original experience, or at one corresponding to any of our previous rememberings of it.
A related point has been made from the perspective of context reinstatement, namely that each time we remember a prior experience, our memory of that experience becomes colored by the contextual thoughts we were having at the time of each remembering~\citep{Mann24}.
We did not test this picture, and our data cannot distinguish it from other mechanisms that would leave the same trace in a transcript, but it offers a natural reading of why a delayed recounting should resemble an earlier recounting more closely than the experience that both describe.

Whatever the underlying mechanism, our account suggests what might become of a memory that is recounted many times.
If each retelling preserves control points and sheds waypoints, and if each retelling then becomes the trajectory that the next one simplifies, a memory might converge over many retellings toward a skeleton of control points, with the intervening detail increasingly implied rather than stated.
Whether that convergence has a floor, and whether the surviving skeleton continues to resemble the original experience or drifts steadily away from it, are questions our data cannot answer.
The possibility seems worth taking seriously outside the laboratory, since the settings in which people are asked to recount an experience repeatedly are often the settings in which accuracy matters most.
An eyewitness who has already described what they saw, for example, is describing a description the next time they are asked~\citep{ChanEtal09b}.
A patient recounting a formative episode across successive sessions may be reshaping it in the process, and a teacher who explains an idea the same way each term may be simplifying it past the point where it still carries what made it worth explaining.

Ultimately, our work points toward a set of questions about how the geometry of a recounting relates to the experience that it describes.
Do the events that behave like control points in a recounting correspond to the boundaries at which neural activity shifts during the experience itself~\citep{ChenEtal17,BaldEtal17}?
Can they be predicted in advance from the stimulus, or are they recoverable only after the fact, from a particular person's path?
Does a listener who hears a recounting actually re-traverse the speaker's trajectory, filling in the waypoints that the speaker left out?
Would causal and importance judgments, collected for this same episode, show that geometric control points and causally pivotal events are one notion arrived at by different routes?
Questions of this kind become tractable once what a person retains from an experience is treated as a shape that can be measured, rather than as a quantity that can only be counted.
Because that shape may be recovered from what someone says, and because control points are identifiable from a single recounting, it may eventually be possible to ask what a student will still carry from a lecture, or what a patient will still carry from a conversation with their physician, before the delay that would reveal it has passed.
We hope that this offers a way of thinking about forgetting as something other than loss, in that what falls away between one telling and the next is largely what the remaining structure already implies, and what survives is the shape in which an experience can be carried forward and handed on.

\subsection*{Participants}

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size.

At the start of the experiment, we asked each participant to complete a demographic survey that included questions about their age, sex, race, and ethnicity.
Participants' ages ranged from 18 to 22 years (mean: 18.91 years; standard deviation: 1.01 years).
A total of 41 participants reported their sex as female and 12 participants reported their sex as male.
Participants reported their races as White (34 participants), Asian (16 participants), Black or African American (3 participants), American Indian or Alaska Native (1 participant), and other (1 participant).
(Note that some participants selected multiple racial categories.)
A total of 52 participants reported their ethnicity as ``Not Hispanic or Latino'' and 1 participant reported their ethnicity as ``Hispanic or Latino.''

We also asked participants their native spoken language; whether they had any hearing, speech, or color vision impairments; and about any current medications or recent injuries that could impact attention or memory.
All participants reported that their native spoken language was English, that they had no hearing or speech impairments, and that they had normal color vision.
A total of 52 participants reported no attention- or memory-relevant medications or injuries. One participant reported sustaining a concussion more than 3 years prior to the experiment and was not excluded from our dataset.

Additionally, before each of the two experiment sessions, we asked participants about their sleep, coffee consumption, and level of alertness. 
Participants reported having slept between 5 and 11 hours the night prior to session 1 (mean: 7.37 hours; standard deviation: 1.45 hours) and between 5 and 11 hours the night prior to session 2 (mean: 7.39 hours; standard deviation: 1.23 hours).

\subsection*{Experiment}\label{subsec:experiment}

The experiment took place over two sessions lasting approximately one hour each, separated by 6--8 days (mean: 6.76 days; standard deviation: 0.73 days).
Prior to the start of the experiment, participants were assigned to one of two groups (\textit{A} or \textit{B}), alternating based on enrollment order.
During session 1, all participants first viewed the pilot episode of the FX series \textit{Atlanta} (``The Big Bang''; duration: 24 min 15 s).
Immediately after viewing, participants were given up to one hour to recount what happened in the episode to the best of their abilities.
Following~\cite{ChenEtal17}, participants were instructed to describe the episode in as much detail as possible and to try to speak for a minimum of 10 minutes.
They were told to try to recount the episode's events in their original order, but that they were free to go back and describe earlier events should they realize they had missed something.
After the immediate recall phase, participants were then given up to 5 minutes to predict what they thought would happen in the show's next episode.

Upon returning for session 2, all participants were again given up to one hour to recount the \textit{Atlanta} episode they had viewed the week prior.
Following the delayed recall phase, participants in Group \textit{A} then viewed the second episode of \textit{Atlanta} (``Streets on Lock''; duration: 21 min 57 s) while participants in Group \textit{B} instead viewed the pilot episode of the FOX series \textit{Arrested Development} (``Pilot''; duration: 20 min 37 s).
Both groups were then given up to one hour to recount the just-watched episode in the same manner and given the same instructions as in session 1.
The experiment was conducted in a light- and sound-attenuated testing room, with videos displayed using a 27-inch iMac desktop computer (resolution: 5120 $\times$ 2880) and sound presented using the computer's built-in speakers.
Participants were given the ability to end each recall phase at any point after the first 10 minutes by pressing a button on the keyboard when they felt they had recalled everything they could.
No participants' recalls were truncated by the 1-hour time limit (i.e., all participants chose to end each recall phase within 60 minutes). 
We implemented the experiment using the psiTurk platform~\citep{GureEtal16} and jsPsych library~\citep{deLe15}, with a custom jsPsych plugin we developed in prior work for recording verbal recall audio~\citep{ZimaEtal18}. 
Here we analyze participants' immediate and delayed recalls of the \textit{Atlanta} episode viewed in session 1.


\subsection*{Stimulus annotation and recall transcription}\label{subsec:annotations-transcripts}

To capture the evolving semantic content of the \textit{Atlanta} episode, we created detailed text annotations of the on-screen content during each individual camera shot throughout the episode (mean annotation duration: 3.05 s; standard deviation: 1.80 s).
Specifically, we used ANVIL~\citep{Kipp01} to record, for each camera shot, a set of semantically relevant features. These included a brief description of physical occurrences and characters' explicit actions, a brief description of characters' internal states (e.g., emotions, motivations) and other implicit content, the names of all characters appearing on screen, a transcription of any dialogue, the name(s) of the character(s) speaking, the shot's setting, whether the shot took place indoors or outdoors, and whether or not there was music playing during the shot.
This feature set was adapted from a similar annotation corpus created by~\cite{ChenEtal17}, which we used in developing our trajectory-analysis framework in prior work~\citep{HeusEtal21}.

We then transcribed each participant's immediate and delayed verbal recall of the \textit{Atlanta} episode.
Our transcription process consisted of three steps: an initial manual transcription, a two-pass prose-formatting conversion with a large language model, and a final manual cleanup and correction step. 
First, we manually transcribed each recall audio file collected during the experiment.
These initial transcripts reflected participants' spoken recalls verbatim, without any punctuation or additional formatting, and including disfluencies, stuttered repetitions, and other artifacts common to unrehearsed speech.
However, since transformer models (such as we used to obtain text embeddings of participants' recalls) are trained almost exclusively on written text, structural conventions like sentence breaks, punctuation, and capitalization provide an important signal these models leverage to contextual other tokens (e.g., words) and construct a representation of the text's overall meaning.
Meanwhile, speech-native phenomena such as stuttered repetitions and filler words (``like'', ``um'', etc.) that are comparatively rare in written text contribute out-of-distribution noise that can be expected to degrade embedding fidelity, and prior work has shown that automated removal of these phenomena improves performance on downstream tasks that assume clean, written-style input~\citep{ChenEtal22,WangEtal10b}.
We therefore used a large language model~\citep[Qwen3.5 122b;][]{Qwen26} to convert participants' recall transcripts to a structure and style more typical of written text.
We used the \texttt{langchain\_dartmouth} Python package~\citep{StonEtal25} to implement this conversion as two sequential passes over each transcript.
In the first pass, we prompted the model to split the transcript into sentences at natural breaking points, add punctuation including quotes around reported speech, remove clear non-word disfluencies (``um,'' ``uh,'', ``hmm,'' etc.), collapse stuttered verbatim repetitions, and mark self-corrections and restarts with em-dashes.
In the second pass, we prompted the model to remove all instances of ``like'' used as a filler word.
We removed filler ``like'' instances in a separate pass because of their high frequency of occurrence in the transcripts and the additional effort to required to distinguish them from meaningful uses of ``like.''
The full texts of our prompts for both passes can be found in \suppMethods.
Finally, we manually reviewed all LLM-formatted transcripts to identify and correct any errors, ensure sentence breaks were appropriate and consistent across participants, confirm that no content was improperly added to or removed from the transcripts, and resolve any ambiguities based on the prosody of participants' original recall audio.


\subsection*{Analysis}

\subsubsection*{Statistics}

All comparisons were made within participant, across the two recall sessions.
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were one-sided where our alternative hypothesis was directional (that participants recount fewer of the episode's events after the delay, and that a participant's delayed recounting matches their own immediate recounting \textit{better than} it matches other participants' immediate recountings).
The comparison between how closely a delayed recounting aligned with the episode and with the participant's own immediate recounting was two-sided.
Spearman and Kendall correlations were two-sided throughout.

\subsubsection*{Constructing episode and recall trajectories}\label{subsec:text-embedding}

We adapted a method we developed in prior work~\citep{HeusEtal21} to transform the \textit{Atlanta} episode and participant's recalls of it into trajectories through a common representation space using a text embedding model.
Briefly, text embedding models take in natural language text of an arbitrary length and output a fixed-length vector representation that captures the text's semantic meaning.
Intuitively, an output vector of length $n$ can be viewed as a coordinate in an $n$-dimensional text embedding space.
While specific model architectures and training paradigms vary, modern text embedding models are broadly trained to output similar coordinate vectors (in terms of cosine similarity, dot product, inverse Euclidean distance, or other geometric measures) for semantically similar input texts, even when those texts share few or no specific words.
We obtained text embeddings for the episode and recalls using EmbeddingGemma~\citep{VeraEtal25}, a pre-trained 308-million-parameter encoder-only transformer model that outputs coordinate vectors in a 768-dimensional embedding space.

To construct the trajectory for the \textit{Atlanta} episode, we first converted our tabular annotations of the content present during each camera shot to a plain-text format.
Separately for each shot, we concatenated the annotation text describing explicit on-screen occurrences and characters' implicit internal states.
We then appended ``Speech:~'' followed by the shot's dialogue, ``Character speaking:~'' followed by the name of the speaker, and ``Setting:~'' followed by the shot's setting.
If any annotated fields were empty for a given shot (e.g., if no character was speaking at that time), we omitted them and their prefixes from the concatenated text.
Next, we used EmbeddingGemma to transform the text snippet for each camera shot into a text embedding vector, resulting in a number-of-shots (478) by number-of-features (768) matrix.
Since the camera shots varied in duration, we then resampled this series of embedding vectors to a consistent temporal resolution.
We assigned each vector to the timepoint midway between the start and end of its corresponding camera shot (in seconds), and used linear interpolation to estimate a vector for each second of the episode.
This yielded a number-of-seconds (1,455) by number-of-features (768) matrix---i.e., a 768-dimensional \textit{trajectory}.

We then created similar trajectories for each participant's immediate and delayed recall of the episode.
We began by splitting each recall transcript into a list of sentences, with the constraint that splits could not occur within reported speech (i.e., if a participant recounted a multi-sentence quote by a character in the episode, we treated the full quote as a single sentence-unit).
We then parsed these sentences into overlapping sliding windows that spanned up to 5 consecutive sentences each.
These windows grew to and shrank from their full size at the beginning and end of a participant's recall sequence---in other words, the first window contained only the first sentence of the participant's recall, the second contained the first two sentences, and so on.
This ensured that all sentences from a participant's recall transcript appeared an equal number of times (5) in their sequence of sliding windows.
Finally, we used EmbeddingGemma to transform each sliding window into a text embedding vector.
Repeated for all recall transcripts, this yielded a separate trajectory characterizing each participant's recall from each of the two experiment sessions. The number of samples in participants' recall trajectories (i.e., the number of sliding windows parsed from their recall transcripts) ranged from 61 to 308 (mean: 136.68; standard deviation: 49.70).

\subsubsection*{Relating episode and recall embedding vectors}

In prior work using our trajectory-analysis framework~\citep[e.g.,][]{HeusEtal21,Mann21a,MannEtal22,RaccEtal24,FitzEtal26}, embeddings of the stimulus and participants' responses were constructed using a topic model~\citep[Latent Dirichlet Allocation;][]{BleiEtal03} trained solely on the stimulus content.
Here we elected to instead use a pre-trained transformer-based text embedding model due to the richer and more nuanced textual information these models are able to capture. (For example, these context-sensitive models can disambiguate the texts ``Earn looked at Alfred'' and ``Alfred looked at Earn'' in their representations, whereas bag-of-words models like LDA would represent the two identically.)
However, these richer representations also introduce certain geometric nuisances we needed to account for in order to effectively relate the contents of participants' recalls to that of the episode they viewed.
First, because modern transformer models are pre-trained on large and diverse text corpora, the representational capacities of their embedding spaces span an enormous breadth of semantic content.
Consequently, in preliminary analyses we found that the embeddings of our annotations and participants' recalls (which were all ``about'' the events, themes, and characters of a single \textit{Atlanta} episode) were strongly anisotropic---i.e., they all occupied the same relatively narrow angular region of the model's overall embedding space.
Additionally, because transformer models are sensitive to the syntactic structure of text, their embedding representations carry latent information about the text's linguistic register in addition to its semantic content.
Accordingly, we found there was a consistent angular offset between embeddings of the episode annotations and those of participants' recalls, reflecting the fact that the annotations comprise grammatically well-structured written text while the recalls comprise transcripts of casual unrehearsed speech.

We corrected for these representational artifacts by mean-centering the episode and recall trajectories before performing analyses that involved measuring similarities between embedding vectors within or across trajectories.
Conceptually, mean-centering a given set of embedding vectors (i.e., subtracting the across-vector average from each individual vector) serves to remove representations that are common across all vectors under consideration, and instead highlight representations that \textit{vary} between them.
For example, consider the sequence of embedding vectors comprising the trajectory of the \textit{Atlanta} episode.
Common across vectors within this sequence will be latent signals driven by how we chose to format the annotation text passed to the embedding model, the annotator's particular idiolect (i.e., idiosyncratic vocabulary and writing style), and any high-level overarching themes of the episode.
\textit{Not} common across these vectors are the finer-grained semantic representations that distinguish different events, scenes, and actions throughout the episode from each other.
We found that removing these non-discriminative signals (to the extent they behave as additive offsets) via mean-centering helped reduce the embeddings' anisotropy (i.e., increased the range of measured between-vector similarities) and improved our ability to segment the episode trajectory into meaningful events.
Similarly, mean-centering both the episode and recalls before relating their embedding representations helped mitigate broad-scale representational differences driven by their diverging registers, and enabled us to better relate them based on event-specific, content-driven patterns.

Our approach to mean-centering the recalls was guided by the particular latent representation we wanted to remove versus retain.
In segmenting each participant's recall trajectory from each session into events (see \nameref{subsec:eventseg}), our goal was to discriminate between embedding vectors strictly within a single recall sequence in order to identify temporally transient content-related patterns.
We therefore centered each recall trajectory (in addition to the episode) on its own centroid to remove non-discriminative representations (e.g., register, broad-scale thematic content) and maximize the contrast between vectors within that single sequence.
However, the analyses shown in Figures \textbf{REF TO DTW FIG} and \textbf{REF TO RDP FIG} entailed comparing sequences of recall events not only to the episode, but also to each other, both within- and across-participants, to examine whether within-participant similarities tended to exceed across-participant similarities. 
Here our goal was to remove representational artifacts that could work to bias our results towards significance.
Specifically, participants' idiolects (i.e., particular speaking styles and sets of words with which they desecribe the episode) will influence the embedding representations of their recalls in ways that tend to be similar between their immediate and delayed recalls, but different across participants.
To correct for this confound, we jointly centered each participant's set of immediate and delayed recall events on the two sequences' shared centroid (i.e., the average vector across all events from both sessions). 
This served to reduce the recall embeddings' anisotropy (by removing signals driven by high-level themes of the episode they recalled) and mitigate the effects of the spoken-recall register and participant-specific idiolect (both of which are shared between a participant's recalls from the two sessions) while preserving any genuine drift in the high-level content of each participant's recall over the delay (which per-session mean-centering would instead discard).


\subsubsection*{Event-segmentation}\label{subsec:eventseg}

After constructing trajectories for the episode and each participant's immediate and delayed recall, we segmented each trajectory into a sequence of discrete events using a hidden Markov model~\citep[HMM;][]{BaumPetr66,Rabi89} for the episode, and an HMM-structured decoding procedure, described below, for the recalls.
Given a number of states $K$, an HMM models an observed time series as a progression through $K$ hidden states, each of which has a characteristic signature in the data (i.e., a pattern that timepoints belonging to that state tend to exhibit) and tends to persist across timepoints with occasional transitions rather than fluctuating freely.
Because states persist, inferring the most probable state at each timepoint segments the observed time series into a sequence of events---i.e., spans of contiguous timepoints with the same most-probable state.

We segmented the episode trajectory into events using the HMM implemented in the BrainIAK toolbox~\citep{KumaEtal21}, which alternates between estimating a mean pattern for each of $K$ states and re-inferring the segmentation from those patterns by forward-backward inference~\citep{BaumEtal70}, with the added constraint that each state is visited exactly once in a strict left-to-right order~\citep{BaldEtal17}.
Only the states' mean patterns are learned; the transition probabilities are fixed in closed form by $K$ and the number of timepoints, giving every state the same expected duration.
We enabled the toolbox's split-merge option (five proposals per fitting iteration), which allows the fit to redistribute event boundaries and yields better fits when events differ substantially in length~\citep{KumaEtal21}.
To select an appropriate number of states (i.e., events) for the episode, we used an optimization procedure we developed in prior work~\citep{HeusEtal21} that aims to identify the single timescale of event representations emphasized most heavily in the episode's narrative structure.
We began by segmenting the (mean-centered) episode trajectory into $K$ events for each $K$ between 2 and 50, inclusive.
For each candidate segmentation, we then computed the cosine similarity between each pair of timepoints assigned to the same event, and between each pair of timepoints assigned to different events.
When constructing these distributions of within- and across-event similarities, we restricted comparisons to pairs of timepoints separated by no more than the duration of the longest event in the candidate segmentation, so that the within- and across-event distributions were computed over comparable temporal distances and the across-event distribution was not dominated by distant timepoint pairs that we would expect to be dissimilar regardless of event structure.
We then selected the candidate segmentation that maximized the Wasserstein distance between these within- and across-event similarity distributions.
We found that the maximally distinctive and internally self-similar segmentation was yielded by $K = 20$ events. \textbf{(TODO: ADD SUPP FIG SHOWING WASSERSTEIN DIST AS FUNCTION OF K)}
Finally, we averaged the embedding vectors over timepoints between each pair of adjacent event boundaries to obtain a sequence of stable event-average representations for the episode.

To segment participants' recall trajectories into sequences of events, we developed a novel HMM-driven procedure that grounded the recalls directly against the content of the episode participants viewed and recounted.
Essentially, rather than re-learning separate sets of state representations from each participant's recalls (which tend to be shorter, noisier approximations of the episode content), we leveraged the state representations already learned from the episode to identify transitions between the episode events a given participant was (most probably) describing at a given point in time.
First, we mean-centered the sequence of event-average embedding vectors for the episode, and each participant's sequence of per-window embedding vectors from each session, separately.
For each given recall trajectory, we then computed the cosine similarity between the embedding vector for each recall window and the event-average embedding vector for each of the 20 episode events.
This yielded a number-of-windows by number-of-episode-events (20) similarity matrix.
We then treated the episode events as the hidden states of a hidden Markov model with fixed parameters, and treated each recall window's profile of similarities to the episode events, $z$-scored across all window-event pairs, as its vector of emission scores over states.
We imposed an ergodic transition structure (i.e., we allowed transitions from any state to any other state, since participants may recall the episode events out of order, and allowed states to be re-entered multiple times, since participants may revisit earlier events in their recalls) governed by separate additive transition penalties for forward-one transitions and all other transitions.
Specifically, we imposed no penalty for remaining in the current state, a small penalty (1.5 normalized emissions units) for transitioning from a given state to the next (i.e., transitioning from recalling episode event $n$ to recalling episode event $n + 1$), and a larger penalty (3.0 normalized emissions units) for all other transitions.
%We $z$-scored each recall trajectory's distribution of episode event-similarity scores so that these transition penalties had a constant weight across recall trajectories with different lengths and overall similarities to the episode.
Because neither the emission scores nor the transition penalties are log-probabilities, this model has the structure of an HMM without being a probabilistic one, and the sequence it selects is the highest-scoring sequence rather than the most probable one.
Finally, we decoded that sequence of episode-event labels using the Viterbi algorithm~\citep{Vite67}, collapsed runs of consecutive identically labeled recall windows into events, and averaged the embedding vectors over windows within each event.
This procedure yielded, for each participant's recall from each session, a sequence of event-average embedding vectors and a set of labels denoting which episode event each recall event corresponded to.


\subsubsection*{Comparing trajectory structures}

We compared the geometric structures of the episode and recall event-sequences using dynamic time warping~\citep[DTW;][]{Vint68,SakoChib78}.
The DTW algorithm finds the optimal order-preserving alignment between two sequences of vectors with potentially different lengths or sampling rates by locally ``stretching'' and ``compressing'' them in time to maximize their overall similarity.
This alignment yields a ``warp path'' that maps each vector in each sequence onto one or more consecutive vectors in the other sequence, minimizing the total distance between matched vectors.
The summed or average distance between matched vectors along the warp path (the ``warp cost'') is commonly taken as a measure of the two sequences' structural dissimilarity.
We computed the warp cost as the average cosine distance between DTW-matched events so that the cost values would be comparable across comparisons involving event-sequences of different lengths (whereas summed distance necessarily grows with sequence length).
