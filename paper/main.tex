\begin{abstract}\noindent
What is a second recounting of an experience a recounting \textit{of}?
Memories change with time and with retrieval, so each recounting may be shaped less by the experience itself than by how we last recounted it.
Here we use a geometric framework to compare two recountings of the same experience.
We model experiences and recountings as trajectories through a semantic embedding space, then segment each trajectory into discrete events.
We apply our approach to data collected from participants who watched a television episode, verbally recounted it immediately afterward, then recounted it again one week later.
We find that the trajectories of participants' delayed recountings resemble their own immediate recountings more closely than they resemble the episode itself.
We also find that this resemblance is person-specific enough to identify one of an individual's recounting of the experience from the geometry of their other recounting.
The delayed recountings' trajectories were also well described as geometrically simplified versions of immediate recountings' trajectories, in that the immediately recounted events that least contributed to a trajectory's shape were the events most likely to be omitted from the delayed recounting.
These findings suggest that the act of retrieving a memory after a delay draws on a person-specific distillation of the experience, rather than the original experience as it was first encountered.
Further, the distillation itself appears to proceed in a way that best preserves the shape of the original recounting's trajectory.
\end{abstract}


\section*{Introduction}

Suppose you attend a friend's wedding.
That evening, you describe it to your partner, recounting how the ceremony ran long, how the best man's toast was unexpectedly moving, and how a small child wandered onto the dance floor midway through the first song.
A week later your coworker asks how the wedding was, and you tell the story again.
What specifically are you recounting to your coworker?
Is it the wedding itself, as you experienced it?
Or is it the \textit{story} about the wedding you told your partner the week before?
Or is it perhaps some combination of the two?
Further, if the two recountings differ, do their differences solely reflect the ordinary imprecision of putting an experience into words?
Or is it that the representation itself has changed?

An experience is already transformed well before anyone sets out to describe it.
Our brains do not maintain an exhaustive account of everything we encounter, so the very act of perceiving an experience in the first place entails compressing and distorting it.
We attend closely to some aspects of a scene while ignoring others, and we tend to interpret ambiguous moments in light of what has happened previously and what we expect will happen next.
But once an experience has ended, neither the experience itself nor our moment-to-moment perception of it remains available, to us or to anyone else.
Rather, what remains is the product of a further distillation through which our perception of the experience is reduced to something our memory systems can store and later reconstruct.
Which aspects of an experience survive this second distillation may depend on what we attended to; what we anticipated would matter later; what we found interesting; and the thoughts, motivations, and biases we brought with us.
Because remembering is reconstructive, the resulting account often preserves the general ``shape'' of what happened while discarding many of its particulars~\citep{Bart32,SchaEtal11,Scha12}.
The units over which these distillations operate appear to be events.
People segment continuous experience into discrete episodes at moments of change, and those boundaries shape what is remembered afterwards~\citep{RadvZack17,BaldEtal17}.
In prior work we characterized this transformation geometrically by modeling an experience and a person's recounting of it as trajectories through a semantic space, and asking which properties of the experience's trajectory are preserved in the recounting~\citep{HeusEtal21}.

After encoding an experience into memory, the representation of that memory is not fixed.
Information may be lost over time; experiences we have after an event may color how we later remember it; similar experiences may interfere with one another; and we may simply re-evaluate what happened in light of what we have learned since.
Even the act of retrieving a memory itself appears to be one of the forces that changes it, in that a memory can become labile when it is reactivated and must be stabilized again afterwards~\citep{NadeEtal00,HupbEtal07,HupbEtal09,SedeEtal11}.
Retrieval also changes what will be accessible later, relative to memories that go unretrieved~\citep{RoedKarp06a,KarpRoed07}.
The particular way in which we recount an experience appears to matter as well, since people who are asked to describe an event for one purpose later remember it in ways that are biased toward that description~\citep{TverMars00}.
Memories become more semantic and less perceptually detailed with the passage of time and with repeated rememberings~\citep{LifaEtal21}.
Repeated retrievals of naturalistic events over the course of a week also seem to stabilize each person's recall (for example, which details they include) without causing different people's recalls to converge~\citep{MeleEtal25}.
Taken together, these observations suggest that each time we remember an experience, the substance of the underlying memory we are retrieving differs from what we retrieved before.
Further, the \textit{direction} of these distortions appears to be idiosyncratic across people.

How might we compare two recountings of the same experience, when the experience and both recountings take the form of unconstrained natural language?
Recent work has begun to use language models to quantify the content of what individuals say when they recount what they remember~\citep{HeusEtal21,MannEtal22,FitzEtal26,FeneEtal25}.
The approach developed in our prior work embeds the description of an experience, and each individual's recounting of it, into a common high-dimensional space whose coordinates reflect the concepts under consideration, and through which the experience and recountings each unfold over time as a geometric trajectory~\citep{HeusEtal21,FitzEtal26,VeraEtal25}.
We then segment the experience and each recounting into discrete events and identify which event from the experience each recall event describes, yielding sequences of events that may be compared directly across people and across delays~\citep{ChenEtal17,BaldEtal17,HeusEtal21}.

Here we apply our approach to data from an experiment in which participants viewed an episode of a television show, recounted its events immediately afterward, and then recounted them again following a week-long delay.
We are particularly interested in comparing both recountings to each other and to the original experience.
For example, if retrieval always recovers the original experience, perhaps with some independently drawn corruption (noise), then on average both recountings should be about equally similar to the episode.
We find instead that the structure of a participant's delayed recounting is more similar to that of their own immediate recounting than to that of the original episode.
It is also more similar to their own immediate recounting than to other participants' immediate recountings, to the degree that one of an individual's two recountings can be identified from the other on the basis of this structure alone.
Retrieval after a delay therefore appears to draw on a person-specific distillation of the episode more than on the episode as it was first experienced.

Capturing the semantic geometry of individuals' recountings at different points in time also offers the opportunity to characterize the transformations undergone by our memories beyond an initial recounting.
For example, if individuals' recountings tend to retain their idiosyncratic structures after a delay, how \textit{do} the structures of our memories tend to change with time?
There is a particular qualitative change our memories undergo as we naturally forget aspects of experience, implied by common descriptors such as ``fading'', ``blurring'', becoming ``fuzzy'', or ``decaying''~\citep{Thor13}.
But the nature of this change can be difficult to characterize more concretely.
Here we show that this transformation is well described as geometrically simplifying the path through semantic space an individual follows as they recount an experience.
We consider a method for simplifying geometric curves, originally developed for use constructing cartographic maps at different scales, expressing different levels of detail~\citep{Rame72,DougPeuc73}.
When we apply this method to the trajectory through semantic space traced out by a participant's immediate recounting, we can quantify how strongly each of their recall events contributes to defining the trajectory's overall structure.
We find that this structural importance predicts which events a participant will retain versus omit from their recounting following a week-long delay.


\section*{Results}

We asked participants to view and verbally recount episodes of television shows across two experiment sessions, separated by approximately one week (Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm}).
In the first session, participants viewed the pilot episode of \textit{Atlanta}.
They were then given up to an hour to recount what had happened in the episode to the best of their abilities (i.e., \textit{immediate recall}).
After they finished recounting the episode, we asked participants to verbally predict what they thought would happen in the show's \textit{next} episode.
Participants then returned for a second session between 6 and 8 days later, where they began by again verbally recounting the \textit{Atlanta} episode they had viewed the week prior (i.e., \textit{delayed recall}).
Following the delayed recall phase, participants viewed and recounted either the second episode of \textit{Atlanta} or the pilot episode of a different TV series, \textit{Arrested Development}.
We designed the experiment to support exploring a range of memory phenomena beyond those we consider here (see \nameref{subsec:experiment}).
In the present study, we focus specifically on characterizing how the contents and structures of participants' recountings of the first \textit{Atlanta} episode changed over the week-long delay.

To characterize how participants preserved and distorted the content of the episode in their recountings, we adapted an approach developed in our prior work that casts experiences and memories as \textit{trajectories} unfolding in a common high-dimensional space~\citep{HeusEtal21}.
We construct these trajectories using a text embedding model, which transforms natural language text into a vector representation that captures the text's semantic meaning.
Intuitively, this vector may be treated as a coordinate in a semantic space, in which texts with similar meanings are assigned to nearby coordinates while those with diverging meanings are assigned to more distant coordinates. 
In this way, the distance between two texts' embedding coordinates reflects how semantically similar their contents are.
We created detailed annotations describing the content of each individual camera shot from the episode (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}A; see \nameref{subsec:annotations-transcripts}) and used EmbeddingGemma~\citep{VeraEtal25} to transform this sequence of annotations into a sequence of text embedding coordinates.
We then transformed the transcript of each participant's recounting from each of the two experiment sessions into an analogous sequence of coordinates (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}B,C).
The progression of coordinates in each of these sequences traces out a trajectory, yielding one path through the embedding space for the episode, and one for each recounting (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}D--F; see \nameref{subsec:text-embedding}).

Neither the episode's trajectory nor participants' recall trajectories move through the space at a constant rate.
Both tend to linger within a given region for a period of time before shifting relatively abruptly to another. 
We treated these periods of relative stability as \textit{events}~\citep{RadvZack17,EzzyDava11} and used a hidden Markov model~\citep[HMM;][]{BaumPetr66,BaldEtal17,KumaEtal21} to identify boundary points in the episode's trajectory where the episode content abruptly shifted (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}G).
This yielded a sequence of 20 events, each of whose content we summarized by averaging the embedding vectors across the timepoints it spanned (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}J; see \nameref{subsec:eventseg}).
We then sought to identify whether, when, and how each participant described these different events in their verbal recountings.
We therefore treated the episode's 20 events as a fixed set of states in a separate HMM and decoded the boundary points in each recall trajectory where the participant shifted from (most probably) describing one event in the episode to another (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}H,I).
As with the episode, we then summarized each participant's description of each event by averaging the embedding vectors between successive boundaries (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}K,L).
%This enables us to characterize when participants describe events out of order, and/or when they return to an event they had already described.
In summary, this procedure yielded (a) a sequence of embedding vectors characterizing the events from the episode, (b) sequences of embedding vectors characterizing each participant's immediate and delayed recall, and (c) a mapping from every recall event onto the episode event it describes.

Our high-level goal is to characterize the structures of participants' recountings of the episode, and the degree to which they remained stable versus changed over the week-long delay.
The simplest sense in which a recounting has structure is the one that traditional list-learning studies measure: which of the episode's events a participant recalled, and in what order.
Participants described an average of 16.981 of the episode's 20 events in the immediate session and an average of 16.264 of them after the delay (a reliable decline; mean difference = 0.717 events, $\text{95\% confidence interval (CI)} = \left[0.226,~1.189\right]$, Wilcoxon's $T_W = 199$, $p = 0.007$, rank-biserial $r_{rb} = 0.490$).
Their recountings also comprised fewer events overall, falling from 18.245 events immediately to 17.170 after a week (mean difference = 1.075 events, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.358,~1.774\right]$, $T_W = 321.5$, $p = 0.003$, $r_{rb} = 0.475$).
Measured this way, a week of delay costs a participant roughly seven tenths of one event out of 20.

We next sought to characterize the \textit{order} in which participants described the episode's events.
In general, participants tended to recount the episode's events in roughly the order they occurred, and tended to describe similar sets of events in both sessions.
We characterize these phenemena formally below, but they are worth noting here because this tendency imposes a constraint on the ordering of events that any given recounting is likely to produce.
In particular, when we compare two recountings to each other, we need to account for the fact that the sequences of events they describe are not drawn from a uniform distribution over all possible orderings.
This means that the \textit{absolute} similarity of any two recountings' event-orderings is less informative than their \textit{relative} similarity within an appropriate distribution of alternative comparisons.
Given this, we developed a surrogate-based procedure that tested whether a participant's two recountings of the episode were \textit{more} similar in their event-orderings than we would expect under this shared constraint.
For every pair of episode events a given recounting described, we computed the proportion of times the event occurring earlier in the episode was described before the event occurring later. 
(This enabled us to capture when participants described the episode's events in order versus out of order, and when they revisited an event they had already described; see~\nameref{subsec:precedence}.)
We then quantified the agreement between two different recountings as the mean absolute difference between these ``order precedence'' scores for each pair of events described in both recountings (Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-order}A--C).
We computed the order agreement between each participant's delayed recounting and all 53 participants' immediate recountings, ranked each within-participant order agreement within its distribution of across-participant agreements, and tested these percentile ranks against chance.
In this way, each participant serves as their own control, and whatever agreement the episode's own ordering imposes on everyone is absorbed into the null distribution.
The same logic applies to each of the comparisons we report below.
These comparisons revealed that participants' delayed recountings tended to reflect the ordering structure of their own immediate recountings more closely than they did other participants' immediate recountings (mean percentile rank = 0.639, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.563,~0.710\right]$, $T_W = 354.5$, $p = 0.001$, $r_{rb} = 0.505$; Fig.~\ref{fig:recall-order}D,E).

Both of the above measures (i.e., overall recall and order similarity) treat a recounting as a sequence of discrete items.
While this connects our approach to the long history of list-learning studies, it also fails to capture the richness of what participants actually do when they recount an experience.
As one example, participants often returned to recount already-described events to provide additional detail.
On average, they produced more total recall events than the number of distinct episode events they described (18.245 vs.\ 16.981 in the immediate session), indicating that they ``revisited'' an average of a little over one event per recounting.
But this is different from simple repetition, since the \textit{ways} in which the same event is described across those retellings often differ in terms of which specific details are included, how the description of one event relates to the descriptions of others, and so on.
The same is true of different \textit{participants'} recountings of the same event.
Whereas two participants recalling the same item in a traditional list-learning study implies that they recalled the exact same content, two individuals recounting the same real-world event may differ in the particular details they emphasize and de-emphasize, how they connect it to other events in their memories, and so on.
By embedding the episode and each recounting into a common semantic space, we can capture these differences in a way that is not possible when treating them as lists of discrete items.
Viewed under this geometric lens, individual instances of one participant's event recountings may be quantitatively distinguished or compared (within or across participants, to the original episode's events, etc.).
Further, the shape of a recounting's path through the semantic embedding space (i.e., it's \textit{trajectory}; Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectories}) characterizes how the recounting unfolds over time, and how each recounted event relates to each other.

In order to compare and contrast these trajectories' shapes, we used dynamic time warping~\citep{Vint68,SakoChib78}.
Intuitively, dynamic time warping finds the optimal alignment between two trajectories by locally ``stretching'' and ``compressing'' them to map each point along one trajectory onto one or more consecutive points along the other, while preserving both trajectories' temporal orderings (see~\nameref{subsec:dtw}).
The quality of this optimal alignment is summarized by its ``warp cost'', or the residual distance between matched points along the trajectories after bringing them into optimal alignment.
In other words, the warp cost for a pair of trajectories reflects how closely they can be aligned by temporally stretching and compressing them, such that two trajectories that trace out the same shape will have a low warp cost even if they sample that shape at different resolutions.
Two trajectories that trace out different shapes, meanwhile, cannot be coerced into as close an alignment and will exhibit a higher warp cost.
We note that since warp cost is a \textit{cost} measure, it runs in the opposite direction to the above order similarity measure---a lower warp cost denotes greater similarity between two trajectories' structures, and vice versa.
Taken together, framing experiences and recountings geometrically and applying geometric measures to relate them enables us to formally quantify how similarly their qualitative semantic contents unfold over time.
We next leverage these tools to answer fundamental questions about how memories change over time.

Two sequences that trace similar shapes can therefore be brought into close correspondence and receive a low warp cost, even when they unfold at very different rates.

We began our study with the question of what a delayed recounting is a recounting \textit{of}.
With the above tools in hand, we can now distinguish between two reasonable hypotheses.
One hypothesis (Fig.~\ref{fig:hypotheses}A) is that each time we recount an experience, what we produce is essentially a noisy version of the original.
Noise may increase over time (e.g., a delayed recounting may be \textit{further} from the original experience than an immediate recounting), but the underlying representation being retrieved remains consistent.
According to this hypothesis, on average, participants' delayed recountings should tend to align with the episode about as closely as they align with their own immediate recountings of the same episode (Fig.~\ref{fig:hypotheses}B).
A second hypothesis (Fig.~\ref{fig:hypotheses}C) is that each time we recount an experience, what we produce is a noisy version of a previous \textit{recounting} of the original experience.
According to this alternative hypothesis, on average, participants' delayed recountings should tend to align more closely with their own immediate recountings than with the episode itself (Fig.~\ref{fig:hypotheses}D).

Within our framework, distinguishing between the two accounts illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hypotheses} becomes a matter of comparing the warp costs between participants' delayed recountings and their own immediate recountings to those between their delayed recountings and the episode itself.
If retrieving a memory after a delay recovers the original experience (Hypothesis 1), then a participant's delayed recounting should align with the episode at least as closely as it aligns with their own earlier recounting of it.
If, however, retrieving a memory after a delay recovers a previous recounting of the experience (Hypothesis 2), then a participant's delayed recounting should align more closely with their own immediate recounting than with the episode itself.
We found clear support for this second hypothesis.
In particular, participants' delayed recountings aligned more closely with their own immediate recountings (mean warp cost 0.532; Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}A) than with the episode itself (0.654; mean difference = 0.122, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.104,~0.139\right]$, $T_W = 1$, $p < 0.001$, $r_{rb} = 0.999$; Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}B,D).
We found that this pattern was highly consistent, holding for all but one of the 53 participants in our dataset, for whom the absolute difference between their delayed-to-immediate and delayed-to-episode warp costs was the smallest of any participant.
This suggests that \textit{what} participants recounted after a week is better characterized as a recounting of their own (previous) immediate recounting than as a recounting of the actual original episode that they experienced.

That delayed recountings more closely resemble immediate recountings than the original episode suggests that the memories reflected by those accountings might drift over time.
Accordingly, we found that participants' recountings came to align less closely with the original episode following the week-long delay (mean immediate-episode warp cost = 0.626; mean delayed-episode warp cost = 0.654; mean difference = 0.028, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.013,~0.042\right]$, $T_W = 288$, $p < 0.001$, $r_{rb} = 0.598$; Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}B,C,E).
We next asked whether the nature of this drift was idiosyncratic to each participant or potentially reflected more general patterns shared across people.
To answer this question, we aligned each participant's delayed recounting to their own immediate recounting (Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}A), and to all 52 other participants' immediate recountings (Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}F), and compared the resulting warp costs.
As in our order similarity analysis, we ranked the warp cost for each within-participant alignment within its corresponding distribution of warp costs for across-participant alignments, and tested these percentile ranks against chance.
We found that participants' delayed recountings aligned reliably more closely with their own immediate recountings than with anyone else's (mean percentile rank = 0.971, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.950,~0.987\right]$, $T_W = 0$, $p < 0.001$, $r_{rb} = 1.000$; Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}G,H).
The same held when we ran the comparison in the opposite direction, aligning each immediate recounting to the full set of delayed recountings (mean percentile rank = 0.923, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.874,~0.965\right]$, $T_W = 11.5$, $p < 0.001$, $r_{rb} = 0.984$; Fig.~\ref{fig:dtw}A,I,J,K).
Taken together, these results suggest that the specific ways in which participants' recountings change with repeated retellings are person-specific.

Next, we wondered \textit{how} recountings changed over the course of the week-long delay.
The 2D projections of the example participants' immediate and delayed recall trajectories displayed throughout the figures above suggest---and our analyses thus far indicate---that a given participant's immediate and delayed recountings tend to be structurally similar.
But our analyses thus far also indicate that the delayed recountings tend to comprise slightly fewer events than their corresponding immediate recountings.

%We know from our analyses above that the delayed recountings contain a little more than one fewer event than the immediate recountings on average, and that they align more closely with the same participant's immediate recounting than with the episode itself or with other participants' immediate recountings.
One possibility is that the events ``omitted'' from participants' delayed recountings are chosen (roughly) uniformly at random from the events they recounted immediately.
We note as a clarification here that ``omitted'' is solely a reflection of the ways participants' trajectories are segmented into discrete events, as opposed to an implication that participants are literally discretizing their memories.
Nonetheless, the segmentation procedure \textit{does} provide a way to approximate how distinct each moment of recounting is from the moments that precede and follow it.
We suggest that the number of events identified in a recounting should be interpreted as a rough approximation of the number of different ``chunks'' of content a participant is describing.

An alternative to the events ``omitted'' from participants' delayed recountings arising randomly is that there are systematic rules or principles governing which specific events tend to be omitted versus retained.
For example, that the trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:trajectories} all appear visually similar may actually reflect something about how they compare.
In particular, suppose we take trajectory shapes (of the episode and recountings) as approximations of the true underlying sequence of ``meanings'' participants experience (as they are watching the original episode) or recount (when they communicate their memories of the episode).
If the ``goal'' of encoding an experience into memory, and of communicating that memory to others, is to preserve the progression of meanings as captured by the experience's path through semantic space, then different events (as different coordinates in semantic embedding space) may play distinct roles---and, critically, differ in importance---with respect to how their presence or absence affects the overall shape of their containing trajectory.
To ground this idea in a physical space, consider, for example, a vector graphic representing the coastline of a country.
The ``true'' shape of the coastline is nearly infinitely detailed (e.g., down to the subatomic level).
But in practice, typically far fewer control points are needed to capture the coastline's shape \textit{at the scale the resulting map is to be drawn}.
Zooming in to a single inlet, one might need many points to sufficiently capture a relatively small area of land, whereas zooming out to a country's entire coastline might use a similar number of points to capture a much larger area.
This idea was formalized in the service of drawing cartographic maps at different scales by the Ramer-Douglas-Peucker (RDP) algorithm~\citep{Rame72,DougPeuc73}.
Intuitively, the RDP algorithm iteratively considers, for each point in turn, how far it is from the line joining its immediate neighbors.
If a point is sufficiently close to that line (i.e., within a specified tolerance threshold, $\epsilon$), then we can remove it without substantially changing the shape of the full path (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdp}A).

We reasoned that analogous cartographic principles might apply to participants' recountings of the episode (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdp}).
To that end, we applied the RDP algorithm to the original episode's trajectory, and the to the trajectories of participants' immediate recountings.
We then asked, for each event, how much its path's shape would change if that event were removed.
In other words, we identified the minimum tolerance threshold (i.e., cosine distance~$\geq \epsilon$) at which each event in turn would be removed by the RDP algorithm (see~\nameref{subsec:rdp}).
Here a higher value of $\epsilon$ indicates that an event is more important to the shape of the path, in that removing it would more substantially change the path's shape.
Note that, although Figure~\ref{fig:rdp}B displays the RDP simplification applied to a two-dimensional projection of the trajectories, all analyses were carried out in the full 768-dimensional embedding space.

We found that ``dropped'' events in participants' delayed recountings tended to have reliably smaller $\epsilon$ values in their own immediate recountings than events that were retained (mean probability that the higher-$\epsilon$ event in a random pair was retained = 0.576, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.519,~0.632\right]$, $T_W = 330$, $p = 0.007$, $r_{rb} = 0.439$; Fig.~\ref{fig:rdp}C).

Following this logic, we wondered whether the shape of the \textit{original episode's} trajectory might also predict which events participants would go on to retain or omit in their recountings.
We applied an anlogous analysis to the episode's trajectory, to predict which events would be retained or omitted in participants' \textit{immediate} recountings.
However, we found no reliable evidence that the original episode's events' $\epsilon$ values predicted which events would be recounted ($\beta_{\text{epi}} = 0.012$, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[-0.159,~0.174\right]$, $p = 0.900$; Fig.~\ref{fig:rdp}D).
This suggests that recounting an experience is different from leading someone else along the same semantic path taken by the experience, in that distortions introduced by the recounting process do \textit{not} maximally preserve the original episode's trajectory shape.

We also wondered whether commonalities across participants' immediate recountings might enable us to predict which events would be omitted in delayed recountings across participants.
We again used an analogous analysis to ask whether $\epsilon$ values from \textit{other} participants' immediate recountings could predict which events another participant would omit in their delayed recounting.
We found that only the \textit{same} participant's immediate recounting could reliably predict which events they went on to omit ($\beta_{\text{imm}} = 0.242$, $\text{95\% CI} = \left[0.065,~0.424\right]$, $p = 0.007$ by participant-blocked permutation).
All told, our regression analyses indicate that the shape of a participant's immediate recounting may be used to accurately predict omissions and simplifications in their own delayed recounting.
But neither the shape of the episode itself, nor the shapes of other participants' immediate recountings, carry reliable information about delayed recountings.
This provides further evidence that memories are shaped by our own (unique) prior recountings, even when the original experience was shared by different people.

\section*{Discussion}

We asked participants to watch an episode of a television show, to recount what they remembered immediately afterwards, and to recount the episode again following a week-long delay.
Embedding the episode's annotations and each recounting into a common semantic space allowed us to represent all three as trajectories, to segment each trajectory into events, and to compare them on common terms.
Participants recalled the episode's events in a similar order before and after the delay.
The semantic structure of a participant's delayed recall was more similar to the structure of their own immediate recall than to that of the episode, and it was more similar to their own immediate recall than to other participants' immediate recalls.
Delayed recalls were also well described as simplified versions of immediate recalls: the events a participant's own path could most afford to lose were the events they went on to drop.

Taken together, these results suggest a way of thinking about what recounting an experience does to it.
If experiences and memories may be described as trajectories through a semantic space, then a recounting is a traversal of one of those trajectories~\citep{HeusEtal21}.
Semantic space differs from physical space in that nothing prevents a narrator from jumping between distant locations, and yet participants' recountings turn out to be strongly autocorrelated in practice, which is the regularity that our event-segmentation approach relies on.
Movement through the space is relatively continuous, so describing how one arrives at a later event from an earlier one implies that the intervening region was traversed in some sense, whether it was articulated explicitly or left for the listener to fill in.
Some events therefore behave like control points, whose removal substantively changes the shape of the path.
Others behave like waypoints, which fall close to the line between their neighbors and may be removed with little consequence, since travelling between the surrounding control points already implies passing near them.
Trajectory simplification provides a way of telling the two apart.
How far a path can be simplified before a given event is removed grades that event from waypoint to control point, and the events at the waypoint end are the ones a participant tended to drop across the delay.
That grading is specific to the person who produced it: a participant's own path orders their forgetting (reliably) better than anyone else's path does.
The episode's own shape does not grade them the same way.
How disposable an event is within the episode's trajectory bore no detectable relationship to what a participant went on to forget, and it did reliably worse than that participant's own path, even though the two paths describe the same events.
What a recounting keeps is therefore a property of that recounting rather than of the material it describes.

That not all events are equally memorable is well established.
Gist and central details, for example, survive delays that perceptual and peripheral details do not~\citep{HeusEtal21,SekeEtal16,SekeEtal18,GilbMosc21}, and autobiographical narratives shed some classes of content faster than others~\citep{DiamLevi20,SuEtal26}.
Memories are already known to shed perceptual detail as they age and as they are retold~\citep{LifaEtal21}, and events bound tightly to those around them survive better than isolated ones~\citep{LeeChen22,AntoEtal24}.
Our results add a change in where that asymmetry comes from.
Centrality is ordinarily established from the outside, by asking raters which details matter to the plot, or by sorting content into categories fixed in advance.
Here it follows from the geometry of an individual's own trajectory, and it is computable from that trajectory alone.
An event is central because of where it sits relative to the rest of the path rather than because a rater judged it to be important.
This turns the observation that some events are more memorable than others into a candidate explanation of why.

Why should waypoints be the events that go?
One possibility is that they are redundant, in the sense that a listener, or the rememberer's own future self, can recover them from the events on either side.
Another is that they are simply weaker in themselves, for example less distinctive, less emotionally charged, or less causally connected to the rest of the narrative~\citep{AntoEtal24}.
These accounts make similar predictions about which events will be forgotten, but they differ in what those predictions should depend on.
If events are dropped because they are intrinsically weak, then a simplification derived from any participant's trajectory should predict forgetting about as well as one derived from the participant's own, since weak events would be weak for everyone.
If they are dropped because they are recoverable from their neighbors, then a participant's own trajectory should do better, since control-point status is defined relative to the rest of that person's path.
Our results favor the latter account.
Read this way, dropping a waypoint is an economy, not a loss.
Events that go unreported are also not always unavailable, since a shift in perspective at retrieval can recover details that an earlier recounting left out~\citep{AndePich78,OedeEtal17}.

The same argument implies that what counts as a control point should be idiosyncratic.
An event's status depends on the path it sits within, and different people who watched the same episode construct different paths through it, so the events whose removal would deform one person's trajectory need not be the events that matter for another's.
This is what we take the specificity of the delayed recalls to mean.
That a delayed recall can be matched to the immediate recall which preceded it, on the basis of structure alone, is a fingerprint in the sense that the term is used for the neural signatures by which individuals may be identified from functional connectivity or from their responses to naturalistic stimuli~\citep{FinnEtal15,FinnEtal20,AndeEtal20}.
The parallel lies in the identification logic alone: ours is a behavioral signature recovered from what a participant said, where theirs are recovered from how the brain responded.
People differ, for example, in where they place event boundaries in a continuous experience~\citep{SavaEtal23}, in how much of their recall structure they share with others~\citep{ChenEtal17,NguyEtal19}, and in the character of their autobiographical remembering more generally~\citep{PaloEtal18}.
Retelling has been found to entrench what each person includes without drawing different people toward a common account~\citep{MeleEtal25}, which is what one would expect if each person is stabilizing their own set of control points.

Everything we measured came from a verbal recounting, and so a reasonable objection is that control points and waypoints are properties of storytelling and not of memory.
We think that dichotomy is less clean than it first appears.
Recounting is the only observable available in a study of this kind, so the two are confounded by construction, and no analysis of transcripts alone can fully separate them.
More to the point, the way a person tells a story is not merely a window onto what they remember, but also an input to what they will remember next.
Retelling an event for a particular audience or purpose, for example, biases how it is recalled later~\citep{TverMars00,Mars07}, and reproducing a narrative repeatedly reshapes it in ways that persist~\citep{BergRoed99,StJaScha13}.
If the function of recounting is to let a listener, or one's own future self, re-traverse the trajectory of the original experience, then communicative efficiency and mnemonic efficiency are not competing explanations so much as the same pressure seen from two sides.

We acknowledge several limitations in the current study.
First, every participant recalled the episode immediately, and so we cannot separate two accounts of what we observed.
A delayed recall may resemble the immediate recall because the immediate recall was itself re-encoded and then retrieved a week later, or because a single stable memory was sampled twice and each sampling was shaped by the same person-specific distillation.
Our measures are symmetric and cannot establish direction, and the design lacks an arm in which the immediate recall is withheld.
Designs that do include such a control, in which retrieved information is later found nearer to what was retrieved than to what was originally presented, offer the strongest evidence that retrieval writes its own output back into the trace~\citep{BridPall12,NadeEtal00,NadeHard09}.
Second, we used one episode, one delay, and one intervening recounting, and it remains to be seen how the picture changes across stimuli, across longer intervals, or across many successive retellings~\citep{ChanEtal09b,LifaEtal21}.
Third, control-point status is defined with respect to one particular simplification algorithm, and a different algorithm might well order the same events differently.
Fourth, our procedure matches every recall event to an event in the episode, so content that a participant introduced but that does not appear in the episode falls outside what we are able to measure.
Fifth, our measure of what connects one event to another is built from semantic similarity, and so it cannot on its own distinguish events that are close in meaning from events that are causally linked~\citep{LeeChen22,AntoEtal24,XuEtal24b}.
Finally, segmenting a trajectory into events is itself a form of simplification, one performed by our analysis and not by the participant, and it sets the resolution at which everything else is measured.
Replacing a continuous path with a sequence of event-average coordinates discards whatever varied within an event.
Any detail a participant lost below that scale is therefore absorbed into our own rounding before we look for it.
Our choice of how finely to segment, together with the length of the episode and of the recall period, therefore places a floor on the forgetting we are able to detect.
A participant whose recounting appears unchanged may simply have changed it in ways too fine for us to resolve.
We take some reassurance from the fact that the same segmentation is fit and applied to the episode and to both recountings alike.
It therefore provides a common basis for comparison; it is not a transformation applied selectively to the sequences being compared.

Speculatively, one way to think about how this might work is in terms of attractor dynamics~\citep{Hopf82}.
Rather than supposing that we hold literal duplicate copies of a memory, we might imagine that laying down a memory carves out a basin in an energy landscape, and that remembering the experience deepens a basin near wherever we happen to land.
Combined with the observation that remembering distorts, this implies that what gets re-encoded on each occasion may not be the original memory, but a distorted version of it.
Successive retrievals might then arrive at a basin corresponding to the original experience, or at one corresponding to any of our previous rememberings of it.
A related point has been made from the perspective of context reinstatement, namely that each time we remember a prior experience, our memory of that experience becomes colored by the contextual thoughts we were having at the time of each remembering~\citep{Mann24}.
We did not test this picture, and our data cannot distinguish it from other mechanisms that would leave the same trace in a transcript, but it offers a natural reading of why a delayed recounting should resemble an earlier recounting more closely than the experience that both describe.

Whatever the underlying mechanism, our account suggests what might become of a memory that is recounted many times.
If each retelling preserves control points and sheds waypoints, and if each retelling then becomes the trajectory that the next one simplifies, a memory might converge over many retellings toward a skeleton of control points, with the intervening detail increasingly implied and left unstated.
Whether that convergence has a floor, and whether the surviving skeleton continues to resemble the original experience or drifts steadily away from it, are questions our data cannot answer.
The possibility seems worth taking seriously outside the laboratory, since the settings in which people are asked to recount an experience repeatedly are often the settings in which accuracy matters most.
An eyewitness who has already described what they saw, for example, is describing a description the next time they are asked~\citep{ChanEtal09b}.
A patient recounting a formative episode across successive sessions may be reshaping it in the process, and a teacher who explains an idea the same way each term may be simplifying it past the point where it still carries what made it worth explaining.

Ultimately, our work points toward a set of questions about how the geometry of a recounting relates to the experience that it describes.
Do the events that behave like control points in a recounting correspond to the boundaries at which neural activity shifts during the experience itself~\citep{ChenEtal17,BaldEtal17}?
Can they be predicted in advance from the stimulus, or are they recoverable only after the fact, from a particular person's path?
Does a listener who hears a recounting actually re-traverse the speaker's trajectory, filling in the waypoints that the speaker left out?
Would causal and importance judgments, collected for this same episode, show that geometric control points and causally pivotal events are one notion arrived at by different routes?
Questions of this kind become tractable once what a person retains from an experience is treated as a shape that can be measured rather than as a quantity that can only be counted.
That shape may be recovered from what someone says, and control points are identifiable from a single recounting.
It may eventually be possible to ask what a student will still carry from a lecture, or what a patient will still carry from a conversation with their physician, before the delay that would reveal it has passed.
We hope that this offers a way of thinking about forgetting as something other than loss: what falls away between one telling and the next is largely what the remaining structure already implies, and what survives is the shape in which an experience can be carried forward and handed on.

\subsection*{Experiment}\label{subsec:experiment}

The experiment took place over two sessions lasting approximately one hour each, separated by 6--8 days (mean: 6.76 days; standard deviation: 0.72 days).
Prior to the start of the experiment, participants were assigned to one of two groups (\textit{A} or \textit{B}), alternating based on enrollment order.
During session 1, all participants first viewed the pilot episode of the FX series \textit{Atlanta} (``The Big Bang''; duration: 24 min 15 s).
Immediately after viewing, participants were given up to one hour to recount what happened in the episode to the best of their abilities.
Following~\cite{ChenEtal17}, participants were instructed to describe the episode in as much detail as possible and to try to speak for a minimum of 10 minutes.
They were told to try to recount the episode's events in their original order, but that they were free to go back and describe earlier events should they realize they had missed something.
After the immediate recall phase, participants were then given up to 5 minutes to predict what they thought would happen in the show's next episode.

Upon returning for session 2, all participants were again given up to one hour to recount the \textit{Atlanta} episode they had viewed the week prior.
Following the delayed recall phase, participants in Group \textit{A} then viewed the second episode of \textit{Atlanta} (``Streets on Lock''; duration: 21 min 57 s) while participants in Group \textit{B} instead viewed the pilot episode of the FOX series \textit{Arrested Development} (``Pilot''; duration: 20 min 37 s).
Both groups were then given up to one hour to recount the just-watched episode in the same manner and given the same instructions as in session 1.
The experiment was conducted in a light- and sound-attenuated testing room, with videos displayed using a 27-inch iMac desktop computer (resolution: 5120 $\times$ 2880) and sound presented using the computer's built-in speakers.
Participants were given the ability to end each recall phase at any point after the first 10 minutes by pressing a button on the keyboard when they felt they had recalled everything they could.
No participants' recalls were truncated by the 1-hour time limit (i.e., all participants chose to end each recall phase within 60 minutes). 
We implemented the experiment using the psiTurk platform~\citep{GureEtal16} and jsPsych library~\citep{deLe15}, with a custom jsPsych plugin we developed in prior work for recording verbal recall audio~\citep{ZimaEtal18}. 
Here we analyze participants' immediate and delayed recalls of the \textit{Atlanta} episode viewed in session 1.


\subsection*{Stimulus annotation and recall transcription}\label{subsec:annotations-transcripts}

To capture the evolving semantic content of the \textit{Atlanta} episode, we created detailed text annotations of the on-screen content during each individual camera shot throughout the episode (mean annotation duration: 3.05 s; standard deviation: 1.80 s).
Specifically, we used ANVIL~\citep{Kipp01} to record, for each camera shot, a set of semantically relevant features. These included a brief description of physical occurrences and characters' explicit actions, a brief description of characters' internal states (e.g., emotions, motivations) and other implicit content, the names of all characters appearing on screen, a transcription of any dialogue, the name(s) of the character(s) speaking, the shot's setting, whether the shot took place indoors or outdoors, and whether or not there was music playing during the shot.
This feature set was adapted from a similar annotation corpus created by~\cite{ChenEtal17}, which we used in developing our trajectory-analysis framework in prior work~\citep{HeusEtal21}.

We then transcribed each participant's immediate and delayed verbal recall of the \textit{Atlanta} episode.
Our transcription process consisted of three steps: an initial manual transcription, a two-pass prose-formatting conversion with a large language model, and a final manual cleanup and correction step. 
First, we manually transcribed each recall audio file collected during the experiment.
These initial transcripts reflected participants' spoken recalls verbatim, without any punctuation or additional formatting, and including disfluencies, stuttered repetitions, and other artifacts common to unrehearsed speech.
However, since transformer models (such as we used to obtain text embeddings of participants' recalls) are trained almost exclusively on written text, structural conventions like sentence breaks, punctuation, and capitalization provide an important signal these models leverage to contextualize other tokens (e.g., words) and construct a representation of the text's overall meaning.
Meanwhile, speech-native phenomena such as stuttered repetitions and filler words (``like'', ``um'', etc.) that are comparatively rare in written text contribute out-of-distribution noise that can be expected to degrade embedding fidelity, and prior work has shown that automated removal of these phenomena improves performance on downstream tasks that assume clean, written-style input~\citep{ChenEtal22,WangEtal10b}.
We therefore used a large language model~\citep[Qwen3.5 122b;][]{Qwen26} to convert participants' recall transcripts to a structure and style more typical of written text.
We used the \texttt{langchain\_dartmouth} Python package~\citep{StonEtal25} to implement this conversion as two sequential passes over each transcript.
In the first pass, we prompted the model to split the transcript into sentences at natural breaking points, add punctuation including quotes around reported speech, remove clear non-word disfluencies (``um,'' ``uh,'', ``hmm,'' etc.), collapse stuttered verbatim repetitions, and mark self-corrections and restarts with em-dashes.
In the second pass, we prompted the model to remove all instances of ``like'' used as a filler word.
We removed filler ``like'' instances in a separate pass because of their high frequency of occurrence in the transcripts and the additional effort to required to distinguish them from meaningful uses of ``like.''
The full texts of our prompts for both passes can be found in \suppMethods.
Finally, we manually reviewed all LLM-formatted transcripts to identify and correct any errors, ensure sentence breaks were appropriate and consistent across participants and sessions, confirm that no content was improperly added to or removed from the transcripts, and resolve any ambiguities based on the prosody of participants' original recall audio.


\subsection*{Analysis}

\subsubsection*{Statistics}\label{subsec:stats}

All statistical tests we performed were two-sided. All tests we performed were nonparametric, with the exception of the logistic regression analysis, for which the model itself is necessarily parametric but we estimated confidence intervals and $p$-values in a nonparametric manner (see~\nameref{subsec:rdp}).
%Because our analyses are performed on scales whose values are bounded and may not be normally distributed (e.g., order-similarity, warp cost, $\varepsilon$-at-removal), 
For all other statistical comparisons, we used Wilcoxon's signed-rank test~\citep{Wilc45}.
Given two distributions of paired observations, the Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic $T_W$ is computed by first taking the set of paired differences, discarding any that are exactly zero, and ranking the remaining $m$ differences by their absolute values (the smallest absolute difference is assigned rank 1; the largest is assigned rank $m$). 
For the one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test, the observations are analogously ranked by their absolute differences from the null-hypothesized center.
In both cases, the resulting ranks are then divided into two groups by their corresponding differences' signs, and the ranks in each group summed (where $W^+$ denotes the sum of the positive differences' ranks and $W^-$ denotes the sum of the negative differences' ranks).
The two-sided test statistic $T_W$ is then given by the \textit{smaller} of $W^+$ and $W^-$.
For a given directional effect, the test statistic thus denotes the weight of the rank-sum evidence in the \textit{opposite} direction.
We note that this means the test statistic's magnitude varies inversely to that of analogous parametric tests (e.g., $t$-tests): for example, in a case where every observation exhibited a shift in the same direction, the value of $T_W$ would be 0.
Under the null hypothesis that each difference is equally likely to be positive or negative, the $p$-value for the test statistic is then computed (via the ``exact'' method, which we use here) by enumerating all $2^m$ possible sign-assignments for the $m$ differences' ranks, re-deriving the test statistic (as the smaller of $W^+$ and $W^-$) for each assignment, and computing the proportion of sign-permuted statistics at least as extreme as (i.e., less than or equal to) the observed statistic.
Because $T_W$ is not itself an interpretable measure of effect size (and its magnitude is perhaps somewhat counterintuitive), alongside each test we also report the rank-biserial correlation \citep[computed as $\frac{W^+ - W^-}{W^+ + W^-}$;][]{Kerb14}, which ranges from $-1$ to $+1$, grows with the strength and consistency of the effect, and shares an exact $p$-value with its corresponding signed-rank test.
The 95\% confidence intervals we report alongside each test were obtained by subsampling participants (with replacement) 10,000 times, recomputing the measure of interest for each subsample, and identifying the 2.5\textsuperscript{th} and 97.5\textsuperscript{th} percentiles of the resulting bootstrap distribution.


\subsubsection*{Constructing episode and recall trajectories}\label{subsec:text-embedding}

We adapted a method we developed in prior work~\citep{HeusEtal21} to transform the \textit{Atlanta} episode and participant's recalls of it into trajectories through a common representation space using a text embedding model.
Briefly, text embedding models take in natural language text of an arbitrary length and output a fixed-length vector representation that captures the text's semantic meaning.
Intuitively, an output vector of length $n$ can be viewed as a coordinate in an $n$-dimensional text embedding space.
While specific model architectures and training paradigms vary, modern text embedding models are broadly trained to output similar coordinate vectors (in terms of cosine similarity, dot product, inverse Euclidean distance, or other geometric measures) for semantically similar input texts, even when those texts share few or no specific words.
We obtained text embeddings for the episode and recalls using EmbeddingGemma~\citep{VeraEtal25}, a pre-trained 308-million-parameter encoder-only transformer model that outputs coordinate vectors in a 768-dimensional embedding space.

To construct the trajectory for the \textit{Atlanta} episode, we first converted our tabular annotations of the content present during each camera shot to a plain-text format.
Separately for each shot, we concatenated the annotation text describing explicit on-screen occurrences and characters' implicit internal states.
We then appended ``Speech:~'' followed by the shot's dialogue, ``Character speaking:~'' followed by the name of the speaker, and ``Setting:~'' followed by the shot's setting.
If any annotated fields were empty for a given shot (e.g., if no character was speaking at that time), we omitted them and their prefixes from the concatenated text.
Next, we used EmbeddingGemma to transform the text snippet for each camera shot into a text embedding vector, resulting in a number-of-shots (478) by number-of-features (768) matrix.
Since the camera shots varied in duration, we then resampled this series of embedding vectors to a consistent temporal resolution.
We assigned each vector to the timepoint midway between the start and end of its corresponding camera shot (in seconds), and used linear interpolation to estimate a vector for each second of the episode.
This yielded a number-of-seconds (1,455) by number-of-features (768) matrix---i.e., a 768-dimensional \textit{trajectory}.

We then created similar trajectories for each participant's immediate and delayed recall of the episode.
We began by splitting each recall transcript into a list of sentences, with the constraint that splits could not occur within reported speech (i.e., if a participant recounted a multi-sentence quote by a character in the episode, we treated the full quote as a single sentence-unit).
We then parsed these sentences into overlapping sliding windows that spanned up to 5 consecutive sentences each.
These windows grew to and shrank from their full size at the beginning and end of a participant's recall sequence---in other words, the first window contained only the first sentence of the participant's recall, the second contained the first two sentences, and so on.
This ensured that all sentences from a participant's recall transcript appeared an equal number of times (5) in their sequence of sliding windows.
Finally, we used EmbeddingGemma to transform each sliding window into a text embedding vector.
Repeated for all recall transcripts, this yielded a separate trajectory characterizing each participant's recall from each of the two experiment sessions. The number of samples in participants' recall trajectories (i.e., the number of sliding windows parsed from their recall transcripts) ranged from 61 to 308 (mean: 136.68; standard deviation: 49.70).

\subsubsection*{Relating episode and recall embedding vectors}

In prior work using our trajectory-analysis framework~\citep[e.g.,][]{HeusEtal21,Mann21a,MannEtal22,RaccEtal24,FitzEtal26}, embeddings of the stimulus and participants' responses were constructed using a topic model~\citep[Latent Dirichlet Allocation;][]{BleiEtal03} trained solely on the stimulus content.
Here we elected to instead use a pre-trained transformer-based text embedding model due to the richer and more nuanced textual information these models are able to capture. (For example, these context-sensitive models can disambiguate the texts ``Earn looked at Alfred'' and ``Alfred looked at Earn'' in their representations, whereas bag-of-words models like LDA would represent the two identically.)
However, these richer representations also introduce certain geometric nuisances we needed to account for in order to effectively relate the contents of participants' recalls to that of the episode they viewed.
First, because modern transformer models are pre-trained on large and diverse text corpora, the representational capacities of their embedding spaces span an enormous breadth of semantic content.
Consequently, in preliminary analyses we found that the embeddings of our annotations and participants' recalls (which were all ``about'' the events, themes, and characters of a single \textit{Atlanta} episode) were strongly anisotropic---i.e., they all occupied the same relatively narrow angular region of the model's overall embedding space.
Additionally, because transformer models are sensitive to the syntactic structure of text, their embedding representations carry latent information about the text's linguistic register in addition to its semantic content.
Accordingly, we found there was a consistent angular offset between embeddings of the episode annotations and those of participants' recalls, reflecting the fact that the annotations comprise grammatically well-structured written text while the recalls comprise transcripts of casual unrehearsed speech.

We corrected for these representational artifacts by mean-centering the episode and recall trajectories before performing analyses that involved measuring similarities between embedding vectors within or across trajectories.
Conceptually, mean-centering a given set of embedding vectors (i.e., subtracting the across-vector average from each individual vector) serves to remove representations that are common across all vectors under consideration, and instead highlight representations that \textit{vary} between them.
For example, consider the sequence of embedding vectors comprising the trajectory of the \textit{Atlanta} episode.
Common across vectors within this sequence will be latent signals driven by how we chose to format the annotation text passed to the embedding model, the annotator's particular idiolect (i.e., idiosyncratic vocabulary and writing style), and any high-level overarching themes of the episode.
\textit{Not} common across these vectors are the finer-grained semantic representations that distinguish different events, scenes, and actions throughout the episode from each other.
We found that removing these non-discriminative signals (to the extent they behave as additive offsets) from the episode and recalls via mean-centering helped reduce the embeddings' anisotropy (i.e., increased the range of measured between-vector similarities) and improved our ability to distinguish different temporal periods of semantic content within a single trajectory.
Similarly, mean-centering both the episode and recalls before relating their embedding representations helped mitigate broad-scale representational differences driven by their diverging registers, and enabled us to better relate them based on event-specific, content-driven patterns.

Our approach to mean-centering the recalls was guided by the particular latent representations we wanted to remove versus retain.
In segmenting each participant's recall trajectory from each session into events (see \nameref{subsec:eventseg}), our goal was to discriminate between embedding vectors strictly within a single recall sequence in order to identify temporally transient content-related patterns.
We therefore centered each recall trajectory (in addition to the episode) on its own centroid to remove non-discriminative representations (e.g., register, broad-scale thematic content) and maximize the contrast between vectors within that single sequence.
However, the analyses shown in Figures~\ref{fig:dtw} and \ref{fig:rdp} entailed comparing sequences of recall events not only to the episode, but also to each other, both within- and across-participants, to examine whether within-participant similarities tended to exceed across-participant similarities. 
Here our goal was to remove representational artifacts that could work to bias our results towards significance.
Specifically, participants' idiolects (i.e., particular speaking styles and sets of words with which they describe the episode) will influence the embedding representations of their recalls in ways that tend to be similar between their immediate and delayed recalls, but differ across participants.
To correct for this, we jointly centered each participant's set of immediate and delayed recall events on the two sequences' mean centroid (i.e., the average of the two event-sequences' centroids, weighting immediate and delayed recall equally). 
This served to reduce the recall embeddings' anisotropy (by removing signals driven by high-level themes of the episode they recalled) and mitigate the effects of the spoken-recall register and participant-specific idiolect (both of which are shared between a participant's recalls from the two sessions) while preserving any genuine drift in the high-level content of each participant's recall over the delay (which per-session mean-centering would instead discard).


\subsubsection*{Event-segmentation}\label{subsec:eventseg}

After constructing trajectories for the episode and each participant's immediate and delayed recall, we used hidden Markov models~\citep[HMMs;][]{BaumPetr66,Rabi89} to segment each trajectory into a sequence of discrete events.
Given a number of states $K$, an HMM models an observed time series as a progression through $K$ hidden states, each of which has a characteristic signature in the data (i.e., a pattern that timepoints belonging to that state tend to exhibit) and tends to persist across timepoints with occasional transitions rather than fluctuating freely.
Because states persist, inferring the most probable state at each timepoint segments the observed time series into a sequence of events---i.e., spans of contiguous timepoints with the same most-probable state.

We segmented the episode trajectory into events using the HMM implemented in the BrainIAK toolbox~\citep{KumaEtal21}, which jointly learns a mean pattern for each of $K$ states and the set of transitions between them via the Baum-Welch algorithm~\citep{BaumEtal70}, with the added constraint that each state is visited exactly once in a strict left-to-right order~\citep{BaldEtal17}.
To select an appropriate number of states (i.e., events) for the episode, we used an optimization procedure we developed in prior work~\citep{HeusEtal21} that aims to identify the single timescale of event representations emphasized most heavily in the episode's narrative structure.
We began by segmenting the episode trajectory into $K$ events for each $K$ between 2 and 50, inclusive.
For each candidate segmentation, we then computed the correlation between the embedding vectors for each pair of timepoints assigned to the same event, and between each pair of timepoints assigned to different events.
When constructing these distributions of within- and across-event correlations, we restricted comparisons to pairs of timepoints separated by no more than the duration of the longest event in the candidate segmentation, so that the within- and across-event distributions were computed over comparable temporal distances and the across-event distribution was not dominated by distant timepoint pairs that we would expect to be dissimilar regardless of event structure.
We then selected the candidate segmentation that maximized the Wasserstein distance between these within- and across-event distributions.
We found that the maximally distinctive and internally self-similar segmentation was yielded by $K = 20$ events (see Supp.~Fig.~\kOptimization).
Finally, we averaged the embedding vectors over timepoints between each pair of adjacent event boundaries to obtain a sequence of stable event-average representations for the episode.

To segment participants' recall trajectories into sequences of events, we developed a novel HMM-driven procedure that grounded the recalls directly against the content of the episode participants viewed and recounted.
Essentially, rather than re-learning separate sets of state representations from each participant's recalls (which tend to be shorter, noisier approximations of the episode content), we leveraged the state representations already learned from the episode to identify transitions between the episode events a given participant was (most probably) describing at a given point during their recounting.
First, we mean-centered the sequence of event-average embedding vectors for the episode, and each participant's sequence of per-window embedding vectors from each session, separately.
For each given recall trajectory, we then computed the cosine similarity between the embedding vector for each recall window and the event-average embedding vector for each of the 20 episode events.
This yielded a number-of-windows by number-of-episode-events (20) similarity matrix.
We then treated the episode events as the hidden states of a hidden Markov model with fixed parameters, and treated each recall window's profile of similarities to the episode events, $z$-scored across all window-event pairs, as its distribution of emission scores over states.
We imposed an ergodic transition structure (i.e., we allowed transitions from any state to any other state, since participants may recall the episode events out of order, and allowed states to be re-entered multiple times, since participants may revisit earlier events in their recalls) governed by separate additive transition penalties for forward-one transitions and all other transitions.
Specifically, we imposed no penalty for remaining in the current state, a small penalty (1.5 normalized emissions units) for transitioning from a given state to the next (i.e., transitioning from recalling episode event $n$ to recalling episode event $n + 1$), and a larger penalty (3.0 normalized emissions units) for all other transitions.
%We $z$-scored each recall trajectory's distribution of episode event-similarity scores so that these transition penalties had a constant weight across recall trajectories with different lengths and overall similarities to the episode.
Finally, we decoded the most probable sequence of episode-event labels using the Viterbi algorithm~\citep{Vite67}, collapsed runs of consecutive identically labeled recall windows into events, and averaged the embedding vectors over windows within each event.
This procedure yielded, for each participant's recall from each session, a sequence of event-average embedding vectors and a sequence of labels denoting the episode event to which each recall event corresponded.

\subsubsection*{Comparing recall orderings}\label{subsec:precedence}

In the analyses shown in Figure~\ref{fig:recall-order}, we asked whether idiosyncratic patterns in the order in which participants recounted the episode's events immediately after viewing were preserved following the week-long delay.
To quantify these patterns and compare them between recountings, we developed a measure based on the ordinal ``precedence'' of different episode events within a given recounting.
Specifically, for each pair of episode events $X$ and $Y$ that a given participant recalled, and where event $X$ preceded event $Y$ in the original episode, we computed $\mathrm{precedence}(X, Y)$ as the proportion of times a recall event matching episode event $X$ appeared before a recall event matching episode event $Y$.
For example, if a given participant recalled event $X$ before event $Y$, $\mathrm{precedence}(X, Y)$ would be $1$. If they transposed events $X$ and $Y$ in their memory (i.e., recalled $Y$ before $X$), $\mathrm{precedence}(X, Y)$ would be $0$. If they inserted the content of event $Y$ partway through their recounting of event $X$, or revisited event $X$ later on after recalling event $Y$, $\mathrm{precedence}(X, Y)$ would be $0.5$.
We computed the order-similarity between two different recountings of the episode as the mean absolute difference between precedence scores for all pairs of events that appeared in both recall sequences. 

We performed a surrogate-based analysis that tested whether the ordering structure of a participant's delayed recounting tended to reflect their own immediate recounting to a greater degree than it did \textit{other} participants' immediate recountings.
We computed the order-similarity between each participant's delayed recounting and their own immediate recounting, and between their delayed recounting and each other participant's immediate recounting.
We then computed the percentile rank of each within-participant order-similarity against its distribution of across-participant order-similarities, and performed a one-sample Wilcoxon test comparing these per-participant percentile ranks against a null rank of 0.5.


\subsubsection*{Comparing the geometric structures of the episode and recalls}\label{subsec:dtw}

We compared the geometric structures of the episode and recall event-sequences using dynamic time warping~\citep[DTW;][]{Vint68,SakoChib78}.
The DTW algorithm finds the optimal order-preserving alignment between two sequences of vectors with potentially different lengths or sampling rates by locally ``stretching'' and ``compressing'' them in time to maximize their overall similarity.
This alignment yields a ``warp path'' that maps each vector in each sequence onto one or more consecutive vectors in the other sequence, minimizing the total distance between matched vectors.
The summed or average distance between matched vectors along the optimal warp path (the ``warp cost'') is commonly taken as a measure of the two sequences' structural dissimilarity.
We computed the warp cost for a given pair of event-sequences as the average cosine distance between DTW-matched events so that these costs would be comparable across comparisons involving event-sequences of different lengths (whereas summed distance necessarily grows with sequence length).

We hypothesized that the idiosyncratic geometric structures of participants' delayed recalls tended to reflect the structures of their immediate recalls.
In the analyses shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dtw}, we tested this hypothesis two different ways, and additionally asked whether participants' recountings aligned less closely with the episode following the delay.
First, we analyzed whether participants' delayed recall event-sequences tended to be more geometrically similar to their own immediate recall event-sequences than to the episode's event-sequence.
We began by mean-centering the episode event sequence, and centering each participant's immediate and delayed recall event sequences by their shared centroid.
For each participant, we then computed the warp cost to align their delayed recall event-sequence to their immediate recall event-sequence, and the warp cost to align their delayed recall event-sequence to the episode's event sequence.
We then performed a Wilcoxon signed-rank test comparing the resulting paired distributions of immediate-alignment costs and episode-alignment costs to assess whether the two systematically differed.

We also asked how closely each of participants' two recountings aligned with the episode itself.
Using the same centered event-sequences, we computed, for each participant, the warp cost to align their \textit{immediate} recall event-sequence to the episode's event-sequence, and compared the resulting distribution against the distribution of delayed-recall alignment costs described above using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Second, we analyzed whether participants' delayed recall event-sequences tended to be more geometrically similar to their own immediate recall event-sequences than they were to \textit{other} participants' immediate recall event-sequences.
Here we used a surrogate-driven approach analogous to the approach we used to compare recall orderings.
For each participant, we computed the warp cost to align their delayed recall event-sequence to their own immediate recall event-sequence, and to every other participant's immediate recall event-sequence.
We then computed the percentile rank of each within-participant warp cost against its distribution of across-participant warp costs, and performed a one-sample Wilcoxon test comparing these per-participant percentile ranks against a null rank of 0.5.

Because either recounting may be treated as the target and the other as the pool to be searched, we ran this comparison in both directions.
The analysis described above compares each participant's delayed recall against the set of all 53 participants' immediate recalls.
We repeated this analysis in the reverse direction, computing the warp cost to align each participant's immediate recall event-sequence to their own delayed recall event-sequence and to every other participant's delayed recall event-sequence, percentile-ranking the within-participant cost against the resulting distribution of across-participant costs, and testing those ranks against a null rank of 0.5 as above.


\subsubsection*{Predicting forgetting through geometric simplification}\label{subsec:rdp}

In the analyses shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rdp}, we asked whether the particular episode content a participant tended to forget following the week-long delay could be explained by geometrically simplifying the path through semantic space traversed by their immediate recounting.
We formalized this geometric simplification using the Ramer-Douglas-Peucker (RDP) algorithm~\citep{Rame72,DougPeuc73}.
Originally developed for use in cartographic generalization, the RDP algorithm iteratively simplifies a piecewise curve composed of line segments (e.g., a high-resolution map of a coastline) to a similar curve defined by fewer points (e.g., a lower-resolution map of the same coastline).
Briefly, given a curve and a tolerance distance $\varepsilon$, the RDP algorithm works by drawing a chord connecting the first and last points along the curve, and identifying the interior point along the curve farthest from the chord.
If the distance from the chord to that target point is less than $\varepsilon$, then all points along the curve between the ends of the chord are discarded, and that section of the curve (which, in the initial step, comprises the full curve) is approximated by the chord in the simplified result.
Alternatively, if the target point is farther than $\varepsilon$ from the chord, it is retained in the simplified result and the algorithm is recursively applied to the sections of the curve on either side of the retained point.
The end result is a simplified curve that preserves the high-level shape of the original, but with all spatial details smaller than $\varepsilon$ removed.
Intuitively, the $\varepsilon$ parameter determines the ``amount'' of simplification applied to the original curve: smaller values of $\varepsilon$ result in discarding only fine-grained details, while larger values lead to discarding more coarse structure.
In this way, points along a path that the RDP algorithm discards at lower values of $\varepsilon$ may be thought of as ``waypoints'' that contribute relatively little to defining to the paths's overall shape, while points discarded only at higher $\varepsilon$ values may be thought of as ``control points'' that contribute more subtantially.

We hypothesized that the differences between the semantic paths traversed by a participant's immediate and delayed recall event sequences might reflect a similar shape-preserving simplification.
To test this idea, we first asked whether the $\varepsilon$ value at which the RDP algorithm discards a participant's recall of a given episode event predicted whether they would omit that event from their delayed recounting.
We applied the RDP algorithm to each participant's sequence of immediate recall events over a sweep of $\varepsilon$ values spanning the range of possible cosine distances between the events' embedding vectors (i.e., from 0 to 2; increasing $\varepsilon$ by 0.1 each iteration).
For each episode event a given participant recalled, we identified the minimum $\varepsilon$ at which its matched recall event was discarded from their event sequence.
We then computed the proportion of times an event the participant omitted from their delayed recounting was removed at a lower $\varepsilon$ value than an event they did not (i.e., the probability of superiority, or equivalently, AUC).
We repeated this process for all participants who omitted at least one immediately recalled event from their delayed recounting (48 out of 53) and performed a one-sample Wilcoxon test comparing the resulting distribution of proportions against a null of 0.5. Because the RDP algorithm cannot remove the endpoints of a curve, we excluded the first and last episode event each participant recalled from their data for this analysis. 
When a given episode event matched more than one event in a participant's immediate recall, we used the lowest $\varepsilon$ value at which a matching recall event was discarded, and counted the event as omitted if it was not matched by multiple events in the participant's delayed recall (this removed a total of 38 duplicate matches out of 861 non-endpoint recall events, across all participants).

We then examined whether this simplification was specific to the geometry of each individual's immediate recounting, rather than driven by general patterns in forgetting.
We again used a surrogate-driven procedure, here to test whether the events an individual omitted in their delayed recounting were better predicted by the simplified geometry of their own immediate recounting than by that of other participants' immediate recountings.
For a given target participant $A$ and a given surrogate participant $B$, we computed the probability of superiority for the events participant $A$ omitted post-delay using the $\varepsilon$ values at which each event was removed from $A$'s immediate recounting and $B$'s immediate recounting, separately, and considering only the events that both participants recalled in the immediate session.
We repeated this across all surrogates for the target participant, and computed the proportion of target-surrogate comparisons in which the probability of superiority derived from the target participant's own recall geometry was greater than that derived from the surrogate participant's recall geometry.
Repeated for all target participants, this yielded a set of per-participant ``win rates'', which we compared via a one-sample Wilcoxon test against a null rate of 0.5. 
We restricted the pool of surrogate participants for each target participant to those participants who recalled at least one event in the immediate session that the target participant omitted in the delayed session (in order to calculate probabilities of superiority; this excluded 175 total surrogates across 2,496 target-surrogate pairs).

Finally, we asked whether participants' delayed recall omissions were predicted specifically a simplification of their own immediate recall geometry rather than by a simplification of the original episode's geometry.
We performed a regression analysis that tested the effect of an event's RDP-removability from a participant's immediate recall geometry on its probability of being omitted post-delay, while controlling for the analogous effect of its RDP-removability from the \textit{episode's} geometry.
For each participant, we computed two ``$\varepsilon$ at removal'' scores for each episode event they recalled in the immediate session: the $\varepsilon$ at which it was discarded from their immediate recall event-sequence, and the $\varepsilon$ at which it was discarded from the episode's event-sequence.
As above, since the RDP algorithm cannot remove the endpoints of a curve, we excluded the first and last episode event and, if they differed, the episode events matched by the participant's first and last immediate recall events.
We then multiplied these ``$\varepsilon$ at removal'' scores for each episode event by $-1$ so that they represented ``removability'' (i.e., higher scores $\Rightarrow$ more RDP-removable), and $z$-scored the events' removability scores from each source, separately, so their scales were comparable both with each other and across participants.
We then used these two $z$-scored removability scores across all episode events recalled by all participants in the immediate session to fit a logistic regression:

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{logit}\!\big(\Pr(\text{omitted}_{pe}=1)\big)
    = \beta_0 + \beta_{\text{imm}}\, z^{\text{imm}}_{pe} + \beta_{\text{epi}}\, z^{\text{epi}}_{pe}
\end{equation}

where $\operatorname{logit}(q) = \log\!\big(q/(1-q)\big)$, $\text{omitted}_{pe}$ indicates whether episode event $e$ recalled in the immediate session by participant $p$ was omitted ($1$) or retained ($0$) in their delayed recall, and $z^{\text{imm}}_{pe}$ and $z^{\text{epi}}_{pe}$ are the within-participant $z$-scored removability scores of that event computed from participant $p$'s own immediate recall event-sequence and from the episode's event-sequence, respectively.
The coefficients $\beta_{\text{imm}}$ and $\beta_{\text{epi}}$ thus express the change in the log-odds of omission per within-participant standard-deviation increase in removability, with intercept $\beta_0$.
We note that because the within-participant $z$-scoring removes each participant's baseline and scale in removability scores, the estimated coefficients reflect purely within-participant variation and cannot be driven by between-participant differences in removability.

To estimate $p$-values for these coefficients, we used a participant-blocked permutation procedure.
For each of 10,000 permutations, we randomly shuffled the ``omitted'' labels within each participant's set of immediately recalled episode events, independently, and preserving the numbers of episode events they omitted versus retained in following the delay.
We then re-fit the above model using each set of permuted labels to construct a null distribution of coefficients, and computed a $p$-value as $\frac{r+1}{n+1}$, where $r$ is the number of permutations that yielded a coefficient whose absolute value was greater than or equal to that of the observed coefficient, and $n$ is the number of permutations we performed (10,000).

To construct the 95\% confidence intervals displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:rdp}D, we used a cluster bootstrap procedure.
For each of 10,000 bootstraps, we subsampled participants (with replacement) from the full pool and re-fit the above model using each subsample's data. 
This yielded bootstrap distributions of 10,000 $\beta_{\text{imm}}$ and $\beta_{\text{epi}}$ coefficients, from which we computed the 2.5\textsuperscript{th} and 97.5\textsuperscript{th} percentiles.
