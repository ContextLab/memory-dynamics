\begin{abstract}
Each event we experience in our day-to-day lives undergoes a series of transformations as it is encoded and maintained in memory. Our brains’ distributed representations of memories by their contextual features gives rise to a framework in which temporally distant events may be recognized as related, and into which subsequent memories may be incorporated. In turn, existing memories within this framework influence how we process and remember future experiences. Here, I build upon a computational modeling approach recently developed by \cite{HeusEtal18c} to cast experiences and their corresponding recollections as “thought trajectories”—geometric shapes that unfold in a high-dimensional space defined by the experience’s content.  With these trajectories, I characterize how episodic memory is altered by the passage of time and the contextual congruence of prior experienes. Participants viewed the pilot episode of a popular TV show (\textit{Atlanta}), verbally recalled its content, and predicted how the next episode would unfold. A week later, they again recalled the pilot before viewing and recounting either the show’s second episode or the pilot of a different show (\textit{Arrested Development}). Examining how participants’ memories are morphed over the intervening week, I propose a neural mechanism for the partially time-dependent decay of episodic content representations.  I then draw upon a model of distributed neural semantic representations \citep{HuthEtal16} and a massive online database of neuroimaging studies \citep{YarkEtal11} to map these trajectories and their distortions onto the human neocortex. This enables me to examine how the neural representation of an experience's content influences its inherent memorability.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

In traditional free recall studies, participants are presented with a series of items (e.g.,~words, pictures, etc.), then asked to recall them to the best of their abilities, in any order. The behavioral findings yielded by such studies on recall order and transitions \citep{Murd62a, Kaha96}, temporal and semantic clustering tendencies \citep{HowaKaha02b}, and response latency \citep{MurdOkad70} have given rise to numerous models of memory encoding, storage, and retrieval \citep[e.g.,][]{AtkiShif68, HowaKaha02a, SedeEtal08, ShanEtal09, ShanHowa12}. The functional predictions of these models have, in turn, spurred investigations into their biological underpinnings \citep[e.g.,][]{PolyEtal05a, MannEtal11,MannEtal12,HowaEtal12,FolkEtal18}.  However, the free recall paradigm is limited in its ability to assess episodic memory for real-world events \citep[see][]{KoriGold94,ChenEtal17,HukEtal18,BaldEtal18,HeusEtal18c,Mann19}.  Its inherently discretized stimuli, binary accuracy evaluation, and reliance on participants' precise reproduction of presented stimuli clash with the steady, continuous evolution of real-life experiences and the value we instead place on capturing their general gist in assessing our (or others') memories for them.

The emergence and popularization of naturalistic paradigms offers the opportunity to create and refine new context-driven models of episode perception and memory that better reflect the use of these systems in our day-to-day lives. In turn, behavioral predictions stemming from such improved models better inform and constrain attempts to identify underlying structures and networks.  For example, a number of these models \citep[e.g.,][]{ChenEtal17} have motivated imaging studies that have recovered neural representation of ongoing events at multiple, hierarchical timescales, largely based in networks of regions including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), medial temporal lobe (MTL), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC).  These multi-timescale episodic memory findings join similar discoveries for reinforcement learning \citep[e.g.,][]{BernEtal11, BromEtal10c}, motor learning \citep[e.g.,][]{FusiEtal07, KimEtal15}, and spatial memory \citep[e.g.,][]{StraEtal14} in support of the long-standing challenge against the functional separation between memory and ongoing information processing (see \citealp{HassEtal15} for review).  Yet, memory for an experience is rarely as detailed as real-time perception, and its quality tends to degrade with time.  It therefore reasons that a mechanism exists for transforming and compressing neural representations of episodic content both during and after its initial processing.

The rich complexity and fluidity of real-world experiences (as well as the numerous idiosyncratic ways they may later be recounted) make their content far more difficult to quantify and analyze than the simple stimuli and responses native to list-learning studies.  Previous naturalistic paradigms have turned to approximating these experiences by either synthesizing and tightly regulating their temporal unfolding \citep[e.g.,][]{LernEtal11} or disregarding their content altogether and using state changes in shared neural responses to infer event transitions \citep[e.g.,][]{HowaEtal14,SimoEtal16,BaldEtal17}.

\cite{HeusEtal18c} recently developed a computational model that can capture the explicit moment-by-moment content of a naturalistic experience.  Our framework portrays the dynamic evolution of experiential content as a geometric trajectory through a representational space defined by the experience's contextual features.  Verbal recollections of the experience may then be similarly cast as ``thought trajectories" that traverse the same space.  By assessing the similarity between moments of content and subsequent recountings along their trajectories' various axes, this approach disentangles the overall \textit{quality} (i.e., ``precision") of an episodic memory from limiting ``proportion recalled" measures (such as the simple percentage of viewed items remembered) and enables identification of event boundaries based on the specific content of experiences and memories.

Predictions from \cite{HeusEtal18c}'s model have been leveraged to implicate networks of brain regions in processing the geometric structure of an ongoing experience at multiple timescales (e.g., bilateral frontal cortex, cingulate cortex) and subsequently transforming it into compressed memory (e.g., vmPFC, rMTL, ACC), adding a level of refinement to \cite{BaldEtal17}'s findings.  However, most naturalistic studies remain somewhat limited in that they tend to consider individual experiences—along with the systems that compress, store, and update their neural representations—in isolation from all other experiences.  Our ongoing perception is shaped by internal and external contexts whose timescales extend well beyond the length of a single experiment session (see \citealp{Mann19} for review).  

The human brain represents both ongoing experiences and memories in a distributed fashion across a range of cortical and subcortical regions (see \citealp{RissWagn12} for review).  These distributed traces map onto various extracted attributes (or ``features") of the experience, comprising both the external world's events and the observer's internal state \citep{Tulv72, Bowe72, TulvThom73,Tulv83}.  For high-level cognitive processing of a current or past experience to take place,  its distributed representations must be ``bound" together and integrated to form a coherent episodic percept \citep{Trei96, AshbEtal96, Trei98}.  In the context of memory, this process involves reinstating the cortical representations active during the event's encoding, stored across this distributed network \citep{AlvaSqui94, PolyEtal05a, GelbEtal08, DankAnde10, MannEtal11, Wagn16}.  This segregated storage structure for episodic memories thereby gives rise to our cognitive ability to perceive temporally distant events as related, based on the overall similarity between representations of their various contextual features \citep{AtkiShif68,HowaKaha02a,DianEtal07,SedeEtal08,PolyEtal09,ShanEtal09,ShanHowa10,ShanHowa12,HowaEtal14,Rang18}.  In turn, existing memories within this framework influence how we later process and remember novel experiences, and each iterative updating of this framework causes subtle shifts in its stored content \citep{RaoHowa08}.  

The content-sensitivity of \cite{HeusEtal18c}'s computational model and its geometric framing of experience and memory enable examination of the changes to memories that reside within this framework by quantifying the geometric distortions of thought trajectories for a given experience.  Here, I leverage our modeling approach to study how episodic memories are modulated by various factors including the passage of time and the contextual congruence of existing memories.  Results yielded by these investigations extend prior findings regarding the role of vmPFC in transforming experience into memory and point toward repeated compressions of dynamic content representations as a model for time-dependent memory decay.  I then draw upon a semantic mapping of the neocortex derived by \cite{HuthEtal16} to construct inferred neural representations of experienced and remembered content, and propose a neural basis for the inherent memorability of certain experiences beyond that of others.

\subsection*{Experimental design and data collection}
The experiment was designed and implemented via the PsiTurk platform \citep{GureEtal15} and coded in a combination of JavaScript \citep[specifically, the JsPsych library;][]{deLe15} and Python.  The study took place over two one-hour sessions, separated by six to eight days (mean 6.7 days; median 6.9 days) to approximate the common time span between releases of subsequent television episodes (see Figure \ref{fig:paradigm}).  Participants ($N$=70) were divided into two equal groups. In the first session, all participants viewed the 24-minute pilot episode of the FX series, \textit{Atlanta}.  Following the episode, participants were given up to an hour to recall its events, in order, to the best of their abilities.  Mirroring \cite{ChenEtal17} , they were asked to try to speak for a minimum of ten minutes and told that they were free to go back and describe earlier events, should they realize they had missed any.  Finally, they were given up to five minutes to predict what they thought would happen in the next episode.  In the second session, all participants were again given up to one hour to recall the episode viewed the prior week.  Group A then viewed the second episode of \textit{Atlanta}, while group B viewed the pilot of \textit{Arrested Development}.  Both groups finished by recalling the newly watched episode in the same manner.  Eleven subjects' data were discarded for various reasons prior to processing and analysis: five did not follow the provided instructions, two did not complete the task, two did not return for the second session, one dropped out due to illness, and one was unable to complete the experiment due to technical issues.  The data I analyzed were from the remaining 59 participants (30 in group A; 29 in group B).

\subsection*{Stimulus annotation and audio transcription}
The ability of \cite{HeusEtal18c}'s approach to model the explicit content of a naturalistic experience relies on the availability of detailed, hand-generated annotations that characterize the experience's evolving content.  To create the annotation corpus for each television episode, I used ANVIL \citep{Kipp01}, along with a custom XML specification file to record the following features for every camera shot (see Figure \ref{fig:schematic}A for partial example):

\singlespacing
\begin{itemize}
	\item A brief description of physical occurrences and characters’ actions (\textit{``Narrative details: external events"})
	\item A brief description of characters’ emotional states and various subtextual implications (\textit{``Narrative details: internal states"})
	\item A list of characters appearing on-screen
	\item A transcription of all dialogue
	\item The name of the character speaking
	\item The shot’s setting
	\item Whether the shot took place indoors or outdoors
	\item The presence or absence of music
\end{itemize}
\doublespacing

These features were chosen both to be consistent with and to improve upon prior work with a similar set of stimulus annotations generated by \cite{ChenEtal17}.  Prior analyses carried out on \cite{ChenEtal17}'s annotation set in conjunction with \cite{HeusEtal18c}'s modeling approach revealed that certain features (e.g., what text appears on screen) contributed little-to-no structure to the stimulus trajectory.  Further, some features (e.g., name of the character in focus and of the character speaking) were found to be highly redundant with each other, while others (e.g., the camera angle) had minimal impact on evaluations of memory quality.  I chose to separate descriptions of physical actions from internal emotions and subtextual implications both to acknowledge that not all content is presented equally explicitly, and to enable future investigations by myself or others into potential differences in prioritization of these features in processing and memory. 

All participant audio was transcribed using the Google Cloud Speech-to-Text API \citep{HalpEtal16}, called from \cite{HeusEtal17b}'s free recall analysis and visualization package, \texttt{Quail}.  In a previous proof-of-concept study \citep{ZimaEtal18}, we found this particular automatic transcriber to be an adequate stand-in for manual transcription of verbal response data, and as this data set amounted to approximately 50 hours of audio, I elected to use this approach.  One significant feature of the Google transcription API is the option to bias the speech decoder toward certain words or phrases.  For each audio file to be transcribed, I seeded the decoder with the union of words present in the hand-generated annotations of the reference episode (removing English stop-words, non-alphabetic characters, plurals, and possessives).  Because our prior work analyzed the quality of the Google transcriber in the context of a simple list-learning study, it was prudent to verify the decoding algorithm's ability to return sufficiently accurate transcripts of natural language.  Prior to transcribing the full data set, I tested the accuracy of the transcriber on a subset of audio files from five randomly chosen participants.  I transcribed each test file both using the Google transcription API and by hand (typed), and constructed topic trajectories from each transcription method (see \hyperref[sec:topicmodeling]{\textit{Dynamic content modeling}} for details).  On average, the automatically and manually generated recall trajectories were similar across timepoints (Pearson's $r=0.63, SEM=0.06$) and their similarities to the corresponding episode trajectory (a rough estimate of recall accuracy) differed by 2.02\% (manual: Pearson's $r=0.63$; automatic: Pearson's $r=0.64$).

\subsection*{Dynamic content modeling}\label{sec:topicmodeling}
Building off of \cite{HeusEtal18c}, I created a separate representational space corresponding to each episode.  Using \texttt{Hypertools} \citep{HeusEtal18a} and its underlying \texttt{scikit-learn} implementation \citep{PedrEtal11}, I trained a topic model \citep{BleiEtal03} on the collection of hand-generated annotations for each presented episode.  To create the document corpus for the topic models, I first concatenated the text describing all features within a single shot to create a multiset of words that, together, capture the shot's content.  I then organized these ``bags of words" into overlapping sliding windows spanning 50 consecutive shots, with each window offset from the previous by one (i.e., the first window encompassed shots 1-50, the second encompassed shots 2-51, etc.).
% move defining topic models and discussion of them to intro?

Training a topic model on the resulting document corpus entailed multiple steps.  First, I fit an instance of \texttt{scikit-learn}'s \texttt{CountVectorizer} class to the union of words across all annotation sliding windows.  I used this vectorizer to subsequently transform the windows into a series of vectors denoting the number of times each word appeared in each window.  I then fit a \texttt{LatentDirichletAllocation} instance to these vectors, yielding a number-of-annotations by number-of-topics matrix.  This matrix described the trajectory of the episode's evolving content through its self-defined representational space.  Along this trajectory, the coordinates at each point reflected the weighted combination of topics present in the content at that moment in time. 

Within this framework, enabling comparison between the trajectory of an episode's content and that of participants' recalls or predictions requires that the trajectories share two features: the space in which they are defined and their temporal dimension.  To facilitate the first, I similarly parsed participants' transcripts into overlapping sliding windows (of 200 words; see note below) and used the \texttt{CountVectorizer} instance fit to the corresponding episode's vocabulary to create word-count vectors. I then used the \texttt{LatentDirichletAllocation} instance already trained on that episode's content to transform the recall/prediction windows into the same representational space as the reference episode.  In order to standardize the lengths of a given episode and each participant's recall/prediction for it, I interpolated the time series of the episode trajectory to a resolution of one second (using the time at the midpoint of each shot as a reference; Figure \ref{fig:schematic}C) and resampled all participants' trajectories to the same length (Figure \ref{fig:schematic}D).  

Note: as part of my topic modeling procedure, I chose to inherit certain parameters from \cite{HeusEtal18c}, specifically the episode and recall sliding window lengths and the number of topics passed to the model.  We performed a grid search over these parameters, choosing a combination that resulted in the strongest correspondance between model- and human-generated memory quality scores (50-annotation stimulus windows, 10-sentence recall windows, and 100 topics).  As the decoding algorithm I used does not identify sentence breaks, I set my recall window size based on the average number of words in \cite{HeusEtal18c}'s recall windows.

\subsection*{Event segmentation and episode-recall mapping}\label{sec:eventseg}
Next, I used Hidden Markov Models \citep[``HMMs";][]{Rabi89,BaldEtal17} to segment the continuous episode, recall, and prediction trajectories into $K$ discrete ``events" (Figure \ref{fig:schematic}E, F; see Figures \ref{fig:a1corrmats}, \ref{fig:delcorrmats}, \ref{fig:a2corrmats}, and \ref{fig:adcorrmats} for each individual's recall events).  Given a series of real-life events, the content within an event tends to be highly self-similar (i.e., revolving around a specific set of people, actions, or ideas) and dissimilar to other, separate events.  For each trajectory, I searched over a range of possible $K$-values (2-50) to identify the partitions that created maximally distinct events.  The optimal $K$-values were selected based on the maximum ratio of within-event content similarity to across-event content similarity, given by the expression
\[
  \argmax_K \left[\frac{a}{b} - \frac{K}{\alpha}\right].
\]
Here, $a$ represents the average correlation between topic vectors at timepoints within an event, $b$ represents the average correlation between topic vectors across different events, and $\alpha$ represents a normalization term set to five times the window length (250 shots for episodes and 1000 words for recalls/predictions).  The regularization parameter $\frac{K}{\alpha}$ is necessary to down-weight the priority of unrealistically high numbers of events (as the value of $\frac{a}{b}$ increases with shorter events) and was adopted from \cite{HeusEtal18c}.

I then estimated the content comprising each episode, recall, and prediction event by averaging over the topic vectors contained between neighboring event boundaries (Figure \ref{fig:schematic}G, H).  This allowed me to map between recalled/predicted content and presented content by finding the episode event to which each recall/prediction event was maximally similar, based on their average topic vectors (Figure \ref{fig:schematic}I; see Figures \ref{fig:a1matchmats}, \ref{fig:delmatchmats}, \ref{fig:a2matchmats}, and \ref{fig:admatchmats} for each individual's event mappings).  Importantly, these matches were computed in a non-exclusive way (i.e., multiple recall events were allowed to correspond to the same episode event), in order to account for participants who chose to further describe an early scene at a later point during the recall session.

\subsection*{Trajectory embedding and visualization}\label{sec:embedding}
To visualize the high-dimensional content trajectories in an intuitive way, I used Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection \citep[UMAP;][]{McInHeal18} to project the 100 topic dimensions onto a 2D plane.  In order for the episode, recall, and prediction content to be comparable within the UMAP embedding space, the 2D plane in which they are embedded must share the same dimensions.  To ensure this, I concatenated the event-segmented episode, average recall, and individual recall trajectories into a total-number-of-events by topics (100) matrix and embedded them simultaneously.  I then separated the resulting matrix (of shape total-number-of-events by two) along the row indices of the originally concatenated trajectories to recover a separate embedding for the episode, average recall, and individual recalls on a shared plane.

To estimate the consistency of participants' recall transitions, I computed, for each participant, the line segments connecting successive recall events.  I next divided the embedding space into a 48 x 48 grid of evenly spaced points and defined a circular area centered on each point whose radius was the distance between adjacent points.  I found the set of line segments (across all participants) that passed through this circle and calculated the angle each line segment formed with the embedding space's $x$-axis. I performed a Rayleigh test on the angles at each grid point to determine whether their distribution was reliably peaked (i.e., whether event transition directions through a given circle were similar across participants).  The resulting $p$-values were corrected using the Benjamani-Hochberg procedure \citep{BenjHoch95}.

The arrows in Figures \ref{fig:a1emb}B, \ref{fig:delemb}B, \ref{fig:a2emb}B, \ref{fig:ademb}B, and \ref{fig:immdelemb}B, C are drawn at each grid point in the direction of the average transition vector through that point (or, the circular mean of angles formed with the $x$-axis), with their length set proportional to the average vector's magnitude.  Arrows at grid points where transitions were significantly consistent by the Rayleigh test ($p<0.05$, corrected) are drawn in blue, and darker blue arrows denote more significant results.


\subsection*{Cortical map generation and activity decoding}\label{sec:brainmethods}
To create the maps of inferred neural representation of episode and recall content, I drew upon a ``semantic atlas" derived by \cite{HuthEtal16}.  Huth et al.~created a high dimensional word-embedding space based on the co-occurence rate of more than 10,000 common words pulled from a wide range of sources.  They then fit regularized linear regression models to each voxel in the neocortex (separately) of seven participants (separately), yielding the mean contribution of each dimension in their word-embedding space to each voxel's activity.  Huth et al.~cross-validated their regression model against participants' real BOLD responses while listening to a narrative held out of training.

Due to the considerable size of the Huth model's corpus, its vocabulary encompassed a high percentage of the vocabulary from my episode annotations (78\% and 76\% of words describing \textit{Atlanta} episodes one and two, and 75\% of words describing \textit{Arrested Development}).  For each of my stimulus annotation corpora, I projected the words shared between the two vocabularies into the Huth model's semantic space and weighted the resulting vector by the output of \cite{HuthEtal16}'s regression model.  This resulted in a number-of-voxels (37,226) by number-of-words (1,022-1,201) matrix of voxel-wise activations corresponding to each word.  By then computing the dot product of this matrix and each word's contribution to each of my 100 topic dimensions, I created a number-of-voxels by number-of-axes (100) matrix of the estimated neural responses corresponding to each topic dimension.  This allowed me to predict an activation pattern for any content vector in my topic space by weighting each dimension's brain map by the vector's displacement along that axis.  I rendered these activation maps using the \texttt{Pycortex} toolbox \citep{GaoEtal15}.

I decoded the activity patterns found by this approach using \texttt{Neurosynth} \citep{YarkEtal11}.  \texttt{Neurosynth} maintains an online database of over 500,000 stereotactically aligned activation maps parsed from more than 14,000 functional imaging studies.  Every study \texttt{Neurosynth} encounters is tagged with psychology- and anatomy-related terms (e.g., "Stroop effect," "motor cortex") that occur more than 20 times in its text.  Then, each of the 150,000 MNI-space coordinates across all activation maps in \texttt{Neurosynth}'s database is tagged with terms from articles in which that coordinate is active.  From these learned coordinate-term mappings, \texttt{Neurosynth} can perform a variety of meta-analyses relating the reliability of voxel activations to the occurrence of words or topics across its corpus.  These tools have successfully been employed for a number of purposes, including decoding of mental states from imaging data, identifying co-activation rates across regions and networks, synthesizing predicted activation maps based on provided of terms or topics, and discerning the anatomical or functional subdivisions of seemingly heterogeneous regions. \citep{Pold11,ChanEtal12,DelaEtal16,RubiEtal17}.

I first created a \texttt{Decoder} to which I could pass my content activation maps.  This \texttt{Decoder} contained a nifti image for each of the 1,335 terms in \texttt{Neurosynth}'s database, where the activation strength at each voxel was the $z$-score from an association test between the given term and that voxel's activation.  The association test result was computed as a two-way ANOVA between term usage and voxel activity (i.e., a positive $z$-score means studies tagged with that term are more likely to report activity at that voxel than those that are not tagged).  Passing my activation maps to this \texttt{Decoder} returned a correlation value (Pearson's $r$) for each term, describing the similarity between my activation map and that term's meta-analysis image.  Broadly, these correlation values may be read as the overlap between areas active in my content-based maps and those comprising regions or involved in processes denoted by the given term.  

In order to avoid the multiple resampling steps that lead to aliasing and decreased resolution in most MRI data visualization packages, \texttt{Pycortex} creates volumes in the anatomical space of a sample subject at a 1mm resolution and uses pixel resampling to project the volume onto a 3D MNI-space surface for visualization \citep{GaoEtal15}.  However, \texttt{Neurosynth} strictly accepts NMI152 2mm space images for decoding.  To convert the NIFTI images generated by \texttt{Pycortex} to the proper format, I computed the MNI coordinate transform using \texttt{FLIRT} \citep{FiscMode03} called from \texttt{Pycortex}.  I then created a 2mm MNI space using the \texttt{SuperEEG} toolbox \citep{OwenEtal18}, and resampled the image to the accepted resolution using \texttt{Nilearn} \citep{AbraEtal14}.

I fit a topic model to annotations of the \textit{Atlanta} episode viewed during session one and with it, transformed the episode annotations, immediate recall transcripts, and delayed recall transcripts into three matrices of topic vectors (see \hyperref[sec:topicmodeling]{\textit{Dynamic content modeling}} for details).  Broadly, topic models take in a corpus of ``documents" (in this case, annotations) and learn the set of latent themes (or ``topics") present in their text.  Once trained, a topic model may be fed any collection of text to identify the combination of learned topics in this new content.  Each of the three matrices created by my topic model therefore comprised columns representing a weighted distribution of the 100 topics present in the episode's content, and rows corresponding to each window of annotations or recall transcripts.  Geometrically, the training step may be thought of as instantiating a 100-dimensional space whose axes are learned topics from the episode.  And given that a matrix yielded by the transformation step contains rows corresponding to successive sections (windows) of transformed content, it may be cast as a series of locations in space (a trajectory) describing the mix of topics at each point in time.

First, I sought to characterize changes in recall precision between the immediate and delayed free recall sessions.  After standardizing the time dimension of participants' recall trajectories to that of the episode (see \hyperref[sec:topicmodeling]{\textit{Dynamic content modeling}} for details), I found the moment-by-moment similarity between episode and recall content by correlating the episode and recall topic proportions matrices at each point in time.  Within topic space, correlation serves as a better similarity measure than simple Euclidean distance, as it is less sensitive to minor deviations along a single topic dimension and instead captures the overall similarity between distribution of topics present \citep{HeusEtal18c}.  To average these precision scores across participants, I used Fisher's $z$-transformation \citep{Fish15} to create a normal distribution of the correlation values, computed the mean, and took the inverse $z$-transform to restore the resulting value to correlation space.  Though moments of event content varied in their memorability, timepoint-wise quality of immediate and delayed recall were surprisingly similar (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}A).  This analysis also suggested that few moments of episode content were remembered more precisely mere minutes after viewing than a week later—most were recalled slightly more precisely \textit{after} a week-long delay (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}B).  Further, there were no significant differences between immediate and delayed recall precision at any timepoint (at $\alpha=0.05$, computed as described in \citealp{Fish25}).

While timepoint-wise assessments are the simplest form of comparison between thought trajectories, they are not necessarily the most informative.  The completeness of one's memory for an experience is not perfectly related to the number of words used to describe it, nor will every person necessarily recount simultaneously occurring actions in the same order.  To account for these potential idiosyncrasies and better assess memory for distinct events from of the viewed episode, \cite{HeusEtal18c} developed a method for identifying event boundaries in high-dimensional models of experiences and memories (see \hyperref[sec:eventseg]{\textit{Event segmentation and episode-recall mapping}} for details).  The event segmentation procedure identifies divisions between timepoint-wise topic vectors where the represented content suddenly shifts (i.e., begins to describe a different weighted combination of themes).  From these divisions, we inferred real-world transitions between episode events and participants descriptions of them.  In prior work, we were motivated to perform this segmentation by the visible block-diagonal structure of the topic trajectories' temporal correlation matrices (e.g., Figure \ref{fig:schematic}E, F).  The dark blocks appearing along the diagonal of these matrices denote spans of timepoints during which represented content is highly self-similar.  Importantly, block sizes vary significantly among participants, as individuals devote different lengths of time to describing specific events before moving on to recall the next one.  I computed the temporal correlation matrix for each of the three episode trajectories, as well as each participants' three recall trajectories, and found the same blocky temporal dynamics (see Figures \ref{fig:a1corrmats}, \ref{fig:delcorrmats}, \ref{fig:a2corrmats}, and \ref{fig:adcorrmats}) to be present in my dataset.  This justified discretizing event memory from the continuous trajectories to account for individual temporal differences among participants.  

I segmented the episode and recall trajectories and identified the episode event to which each recall event corresponded (see \hyperref[sec:eventseg]{\textit{Event segmentation and episode-recall mapping}} for details).  Figure \ref{fig:immdel}E shows that nearly all episode events were, on average, recalled more precisely immediately following viewing than after the delay (the result of subtracting delayed precision scores from immediate precision scores is greater than zero for all but events 0, 1 and 6).  Though intuitive, this result confirmed an improvement in my models' reflection of real-world memory due to the segmentation.  These differences, surprisingly, are fairly small in scale and average event-wise recall precision is strikingly similar between immediate and delayed sessions (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}D).  Again, differences in average precision between the two sessions were not significant for any event.

I then examined the interactions between immediate and delayed recall precision on a per-participant basis.  For each individual, I computed the average immediate and delayed precisions across events that he or she recalled in both sessions.  As may be expected, participants who immediately recalled events with a high degree of precision were also more likely to perform well in the delayed recall task (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}G; Pearson's $r=0.51, p<0.01$).  Interestingly, however, higher performance in immediate recall also led to a larger loss in precision over the course of the delay (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}H; Pearson's $r=0.46, p<0.01$).  This was true in terms of both raw precision decrease and proportional decrease relative to their immediate recall precision.  The same set of relationships surfaced in analyzing the overall memorability of \textit{Atlanta} episode one events: those that were highly memorable (i.e., recalled with high precision, on average) immediately following viewing retained this quality a week later (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}I; Pearson's $r=0.71, p<0.01$), but participants were more likely to discard information pertinent to precise recall of those events in that time (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}J; Pearson's $r=0.36, p=0.04$).

\subsection*{Visualizing recall variation}\label{sec:trajresults}
Panels D and E of Figure \ref{fig:immdel} show that event-wise recall precision was, on average, highly consistent between immediate and delayed sessions, and panel F suggests that across-participant variability in recalled content was statistically similar.  However, because thought trajectories exist in high-dimensional spaces, they may differ from the corresponding experience's trajectory (or from each other) along numerous axes.  Thus, while they are useful baseline measures of overall content recovery, these 2D analyses fail to capture more nuanced trends in memory variation.

To better visualize and assess the evolving content of the episodes and their recovery though memory, I embedded their high-dimensional trajectories in a shared plane using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection \citep[UMAP;][]{McInHeal18}, as outlined in \hyperref[sec:embedding]{\textit{Trajectory embedding and visualization}}.  Although this embedding also gives rise to a two-dimensional space, a major advantage to the UMAP algorithm is the creation of a Euclidean distance-based embedding plane that best preserves the distribution of relative similarities between data in their native high-dimensional space (in other words, nearby points in the UMAP space are also clustered together in the 100D topic space, along their most significant axes).  UMAP's approach to dimensionality reduction is conceptually similar to that of t-SNE \citep[$t$-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding;][]{MaatHint08} , but differs in two ways that make it an arguably better choice for reducing memory vectors.  While t-SNE magnifies the embedding-space distance between nearby points, UMAP better preserves the data's global structure \citep{BechEtal19}.  Because many events were remembered with high precision (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}D) and recalled content was highly similar both across participants and across sessions (\ref{fig:immdel}F), increasing small differences would unjustly blur the distinction between poorly and well remembered events, and expand minor deviations in topic proportions.  Further, \cite{BechEtal19} found UMAP to be better suited to preserving the structure of continuous trajectories, providing a basis for its preferability when working with time series data.

Figures \ref{fig:a1emb}, \ref{fig:delemb}, \ref{fig:a2emb}, and \ref{fig:ademb} display the reduced content of each recall session in a space shared by the reference episode.  In each figure, panel A shows the trajectory of the episode's content, with colored dots representing the location in space of each segmented event.  Panel B analogously shows the average recall across participants, alongside the episode (grey).  Visual inspection of panel B of each figure reveals that while distances between the average recall events and those of the episode vary (reflective of the pattern in \ref{fig:immdel}D), participants were, overall, highly successful in preserving the general \textit{shape} of the episode content's unfolding in memory.  This suggests that while the memorability of assorted events from an experience may differ, human memory preserves both a broad overview of the experience (its ``gist") and those subsections' relationship to one another.  To assess how consistently this shape was recovered across participants, I analyzed whether the direction in which a thought trajectory travels as a person transitions to recalling successive events could be predicted by the location in space of the current recall event.  For each participant, I computed the set of angles formed by line segments connecting successive recall events and the embedding space's $x$-axis.  I then divided the embedding space into a 48x48 grid and performed a Rayleigh test on the distribution of angles passing through each grid point (see \hyperref[sec:embedding]{\textit{Trajectory embedding and visualization}} for details). The blue arrows in \ref{fig:a1emb}B, \ref{fig:delemb}B, \ref{fig:a2emb}B, and \ref{fig:ademb}B reflect grid points where these angles were reliably consistent ($p < 0.05$, corrected).  Note that this test is not sensitive at points where line segments between consecutive embedded events intersect (see \ref{fig:a2emb}B, \ref{fig:ademb}B).  

Panel C of each embedding figure shows individuals' recall trajectories (grey) and segmented events, with the average participant overlaid in black. Here, recall events are colored by their corresponding episode event.  In most instances, the series of events recalled by individual participants follows a rough outline of the across-participant average.  However, the number of events identified by segmentation approach (see \hyperref[sec:eventseg]{\textit{Event segmentation and episode-recall mapping}}) was highly variant.  In other words, the content comprising individuals' high-level gists of an experience as captured in memory was fairly regular—what differed among people was the sampling rate along that gist's trajectory, or the \textit{distinctiveness} of memory between sections of content.  This result is also visible in each individual's temporal correlation matrix (Figures \ref{fig:a1corrmats}, \ref{fig:delcorrmats}, \ref{fig:a2corrmats}, and \ref{fig:adcorrmats}), where the number of segmented events (yellow-outlined blocks along the diagonal) is highly variable.


The high degree of correspondence between immediate and delayed recall precisions (Figure \ref{fig:immdel}) was recovered in these embedding analyses. Figure \ref{fig:immdelemb} visualizes the striking similarity between the average immediate and delayed recall trajectories, as well as the fidelity with which they recover the episode's evolving content (shown on a shared plot in the top left).  However, the nature of recall idiosyncrasies revealed in Figures \ref{fig:a1emb}, \ref{fig:delemb}, \ref{fig:a2emb}, and \ref{fig:ademb} (consistent recovery of a common recall trajectory, but at inconsistent sampling rates) inspired a better method of comparing memory changes over time.  The smaller trajectories in Figure \ref{fig:immdelemb} display each participant's immediate and delayed event recalls, plotted along the episode trajectory (grey).  Immediate recall events are outlined in black so as to be distinguishable from delayed recalls.  Comparing participants' sampling rates between the two recall sessions, I found that after a week's time, the trajectories segmented into fewer events than they did initially (an average of 14.2, down from 17.4) and that delayed recall events mapped onto fewer unique episode events (9.6 vs 11.4).  Using Wilcoxon's signed-rank test \citep{Wilc45}, I found both of these differences to be significant ($T=543.0, p=0.02$ and $T=410.5, p<0.01$, respectively).  This suggests that while the structured unfolding of an experience is preserved by memory in a surprisingly static manner, the loss of memory clarity that occurs with time is due to a ``downsampling function." In the same way that a picture's individual components become progressively blurrier and harder to identify when its resolution is compressed, participants appeared to have lost some ability to distinguish between unique events occurring within a more temporally distant context.

\subsection*{Assessing the memorability of episodic content}

The trends shown in Figure \ref{fig:immdel}A, D, and I suggest that the content of certain experiences are inherently easier to remember than that of others.  This result prompted me to investigate what dynamic features of each episode contributed to their various patterns of memorability.  Because the dimensions of the common space in which these trajectories reside correspond to topics, and because those topics represent weighted sets of words in the episode's vocabulary, it is possible to recover the content makeup of each topic dimension (and therefore, each trajectory's many topic vectors).  To retrieve the explicit content giving rise to each topic space, I first parsed the \texttt{LatentDirichletAllocation} objects used to create them and obtained the weighted combination of words represented by each dimension.  Then, I computed a weighted average of the topic vectors for each episode event, based on their memorability (i.e., average recall precision across participants).  By mapping the resulting vector's magnitude along each axis to the word weights the axes represent, I was able to find to find the overall best- and worst-remembered content across the full experience.

These content-focused analyses offer a number of interesting results.  \cite{HeusEtal18c} suggested that events revolving around principal characters and locations tended to be better remembered than those centered on minor characters.  This hypothesis is broadly supported by my results (see the ``most-memorable" worldes in \ref{fig:a1content}B, \ref{fig:delcontent}B, \ref{fig:adcontent}B and the ``least-memorable" wordles in \ref{fig:a2content}B and \ref{fig:adcontent}B). This event corresponds to an emotionally jarring scene in which Dave, a white friend of Earn's who works at a radio station, casually directs a racial slur toward the main character.  The salience of this scene seemingly served to increment its memorability in viewers' minds at the expense of content involving a character who appeared more frequently but elicited less emotional response.

The repeated exposure of participants in group A (i.e., those who viewed another \textit{Atlanta} episode in session two) to a similar context allowed for an analysis of content memorability under a condition where the characters and themes are already familiar.  The second \textit{Atlanta} episode jumps between scenes of Earn, prohibited from leaving the chair he sits in as he waits to be released from prison, and Alfred's various actions in the outside world.  The oscillatory pattern of event memorability in Figure \ref{fig:a2content}A follows this narrative structure.  Here, both the most memorable overall content and single best-recalled events (Figure \ref{fig:a2content}B, D) refer heavily to Alfred, while the least memorable content is that involving Earn (Figure \ref{fig:a2content}B, E).  This suggests that after sufficient exposure to a core set of characters, memorability is driven by the cognitive distinguishability of episodic content, rather than the familiarity of the subject driving that content.

\subsection*{Neural correlates of content memorability}\label{sec:brainresults}

The previous set of analyses addressed semantic and thematic attributes that may drive differences in the inherent memorability of episodic content.  I next sought to investigate \textit{why} certain features may be better remember than others.  Relationships between processing-dependent properties (e.g., attentiveness, mood, emotional salience, situational relevance, etc.) and episodic memory have been probed for decades \citep{ChunTurk07,TyngEtal17}, but little research has been conducted into a neural basis for memorability originating from the explicit content of the external world.  To address this question, I extended an approach originally conceived by \cite{HuthEtal16}. Huth and his colleagues modeled a vocabulary of over 10,000 words and generated a ``semantic atlas" of activity in the neocortex corresponding to individual words (see \hyperref[sec:brainmethods]{\textit{Cortical map generation and activity decoding}} for details).  Borrowing from their model's mapping, I transformed each of my three topic spaces into a brain map of voxelwise responses from which I could retrieve the activation pattern representing any topic vector within those spaces.  Figures \ref{fig:a1content}, \ref{fig:delcontent}, \ref{fig:a2content}, and \ref{fig:adcontent} show left medial views of predicted activity maps, recovered from the topic proportions for the most and least memorable overall content (panel B) and individual events (panels D and E) of each episode.  

To objectively quantify differences between the activation patterns for various semantic content, I decoded these brain maps using \texttt{Neurosynth} \citep{YarkEtal11}.  \texttt{Neurosynth} parses a massive database of neuroimaging studies and builds meta-analysis images for specified psychology- and neuroscience-related terms, based on results reported in studies that use those terms heavily (see \hyperref[sec:brainmethods]{\textit{Cortical map generation and activity decoding}} for details).  Decoding the overlap of active voxels between these meta-analysis-based images and my semantic content-based images allowed me to compare patterns in the neural representations of viewed and recalled content to established anatomical and functional regions and networks in a blind, unbiased manner beyond visual observation and inference.  

%I validated this combined approach of translating topic vectors into cortical activations and subsequently decoding the maps' activity by comparing a ``baseline" activation map against \texttt{Neurosynth}'s full database of term-based meta analyses. Given that any vector within my topic spaces corresponds to a certain weighted combination of features, such a baseline was difficult to quantify. A topic proportions matrix of all zeroes was not viable, as it would instantiate a brain map with no activity for \texttt{Neurosynth} to decode. However, a collection of results gleaned from analyses conducted thus far enabled me to estimate a highly content-poor map of activations.  Figures \ref{fig:immdel}D,I and \ref{fig:immdelemb} show that on average, memory precision between immediate and delayed recall is strikingly similar.  Further, comparing the wordles in Figures \ref{fig:a1content}D and \ref{fig:delcontent}B reveals that the most memorable content in each session is nearly identical.  In fact, the topic proportions underlying these two wordles are over 99\% correlated.  I therefore used the minute differences between these two vectors to create a pattern of activations that represent less than 1\% of content-related activity. 

%The list of terms whose meta-analysis images were most highly similar to this test mapping was populated exclusively with those related to language (e.g., ``speech," ``listening," ``sentences," ``superior temporal," ``auditory," ``temporal gyrus").  Given that I projected the space in which these content vectors exist onto the ``semantic atlas" mapped by \cite{HuthEtal16}, this recovery indicated the decoding approach was generally accurate enough to use in probing my question of interest.  

%Additionally, the least-similar images were derived from terms pertaining to regions involved in the default mode and in memory networks (e.g., ``precuneus," ``dorsolateral prefrontal," ``frontoparietal," ``retrosplenial").  This incidental finding suggested that these two networks may have been identified by the minimal amount of content-related activity not isolated by my baseline estimation, and motivated the analysis conducted in Figure \ref{fig:eptermweight}.  

To compare the neural representations of memorable content to that of forgettable content, I employed a weighted-average approach similar to the method used to create the wordles in Figures \ref{fig:a1content}B, \ref{fig:delcontent}B, \ref{fig:a2content}B, and \ref{fig:adcontent}B.  I constructed a separate activation map of content-driven activity for each episode event, based on its topic proportions matrix.  I then computed two averaged content maps for each episode—one whose weights were derived from each even't memorability, the other by the inverse of their memorability.  To isolate activity patterns specifically pertinent to memorability, I subtracted the response at each voxel of the ``forgettable map" from the ``memorable map." This causes voxels responding positively or negatively in both instances to sum to near-zero values, instead highlighting the differences between the two.  These difference maps and the top ten terms decoded from them are shown in Figure \ref{fig:braindiffs}.

The existence of such biologically inherent differences between content would have substantial implications for many aspects of learning and memory, and merits significantly expanded investigation.

\section*{Discussion}
Much of my approach to modeling naturalistic content parallels the brain's feature-based processing and storage of experiences and memories.  I first instantiate a high dimensional space whose axes correspond to ``topics," or discovered collections of featurally similar content.  The organization of this space is similar to the semantic system's functional segregation into regions that represent specific semantic domains \citep{Warr75, DamaEtal96, CaraShel98, MummEtal98, MitcEtal08a, JustEtal10, HuthEtal12, HuthEtal16}.  I then cast an experience and corresponding memories as trajectories traversing this space.  At each moment, the semantic content present in an experience or memory is represented by a weighted combination of topics (or geometrically, displacements along topic dimensions).  Analogously, moment-by-moment perception and memory arise from the sum of distributed neural representations (see \citealp{RaoHowa08, RissWagn12} for review).  Finally, I characterize the recovery of the experience in memory by assessing the relationship between their trajectories'  weighted mixtures of active topics.  Likewise, a neural circuit comprising the hippocampus, MTL, and vmPFC has been shown to compute the similarity between experiences and memories via their distributed activations \citep{RangEtal04b, Dava06, WiltSilv07, RichEtal14, SweeEtal14}.

As described in \hyperref[sec:topicmodeling]{\textit{Dynamic content modeling}}, the episode annotations and recall transcripts fed to the topic model are first formatted as sliding windows, whose centers slowly shift across the content's length. The small fractional change in composition between neighboring windows (and thus, the minute difference in represented content at each consecutive point along the resultant trajectories) reflects the well established theory that features of both the external world and individuals' internal mental states tend to drift gradually over time (\citealp{HowaKaha02a,ChanEtal17}; see \citealp{Kaha12,MannEtal15} for review).  Behavioral and neuroimaging evidence has shown that this slow contextual drift is punctuated by occasional, abrupt shifts \citep{ZackEtal01a, ZackEtal01b, PolyEtal09, EzzyDava11, DuBrDava13, EzzyDava14, MannEtal16, HeusEtal16, DuBrDava16, ClewDava17, BaldEtal17, BaldEtal18, AlyEtal18, BrunEtal18, HeusEtal18b, SilvEtal19}.  Sudden shifts in mental context are often explained by the creation of separate cognitive ``situation models" describing the properties of distinguishable events in the external world \citep{ZwaaEtal95, ZwaaRadv98, Radv12, RadvZack17}.  My approach captures sudden shifts in explicit content and defines them as separate situations through the \hyperref[sec:eventseg]{\textit{Event segmentation and episode-recall mapping}} procedure.  The high degree of correspondence between my computational approach and the neural mechanisms underlying the behavioral dynamics I analyzed above enable inferences and predictions regarding these mechanisms that are grounded in strong evidence for their functions.

In \hyperref[sec:trajresults]{\textit{Visualizing recall variation}}, I observed a significant decrease between immediate and delayed recall sessions in the number of events into which participants' thought trajectories segmented.  In both real-time perception and memory, gradually evolving content is processed and partitioned into events at multiple, hierarchical timescales, with nested situation models describing successively broad internal and external contexts \citep{SpeeEtal03, SpeeEtal07, KurbZack08, HassEtal08, LernEtal11, HassEtal15, ChenEtal17,BaldEtal17, BaldEtal18}.  These multi-timescale representations are visibly captured in the recall trajectories' temporal correlation matrices as nested diagonal blocks of decreasing size (see Figures \ref{fig:a1corrmats}, \ref{fig:delcorrmats}, \ref{fig:a2corrmats}, and \ref{fig:adcorrmats}).  However, rather than parse all representations at all timescales, the Hidden Markov Model identifies a single set of event segments for each participant.  By optimizing for the series of transitions that creates maximally distinct events, the segmentation procedure estimates the timescale that participants draw upon most heavily to represent the episode's content structure in memory.

The decreased number of maximally distinct events comprising participants' delayed recalls suggests a gravitation toward longer-timescale parcellations of episode content over the week-long delay.  Creating a greater number of situation models into which gradually evolving content may be divided allows each segmented representation to be more highly detailed.  Prior research has shown that cuing observers to event boundaries in narrative structures encourage shorter-timescale event segmentations, and that this more granular representation of content leads to increased memory precision \citep[e.g.,][]{PettEtal16, GoldEtal17, FlorEtal17}.  In concert with my findings, this suggests that the quantitive change underlying the qualitative act of ``forgetting" an experience may arise from a ``downsampling algorithm" that discards the specificity of fine-grained event representations in favor of refocusing on progressively longer-timescale depictions of experiences.  Downsampling the trajectory of evolving content would serve to blur details in episodic memory over time, while simultaneously preserving the experience's broad pattern of unfolding (i.e., its ``gist").  

An intuitive analogy for this algorithm's use may be drawn from our treatment of computers' ``memories."  Two common digital file types can store representations of the natural world: pictures and audio recordings.  Both types may be represented at range of resolutions (i.e., pixels-per-inch and kilobytes-per-second, respectively).  When high-resolution images are viewed or high-resolution recordings are replayed, their recovery of the real-world content they capture is objectively clearer than it would be at a lower resolution.  However, higher-resolution files naturally require more resources to store in a framework where space is limited, and in practice, one tends to delete old or unimportant files altogether, sacrificing any ability to recover their representations in the interest of creating space for information deemed more relevant to one's current situation.  Human memory similarly prioritizes information by its functional relevance \citep{NieuTaka11}, though its complex, multidimensional storage scheme makes a finite capacity difficult to quantify, with estimates of its upper limit constantly changing \citep[e.g.,][]{PereNiez86, WangEtal03, BradEtal08, Rebe10, BartEtal15}.  However, studies have shown that memory traces competing with retrieval of information pertinent to current goals are permanently inhibited, and their cortical patterns partially suppressed \citep{AndeEtal94, WimbEtal15}.  Episodic forgetting does not appear to constitute a single assessment of relevance that results in a sudden deletion so much as repeated relevance assessments that lead to a gradual decay in fidelity.  This fidelity loss can be detected and visualized through my approach as a decreased sampling rate along thought trajectories post-delay, suggesting that as memories are assessed to be less relevant over time, their representations may shift toward broader timescales.

A central finding of \cite{HeusEtal18c} was that the temporal pattern of vmPFC activation during encoding predicted the compressed structure of participants' later recalls.  Emerging views pose increasing challenges to the traditional distinction between continuous perception and memory, particularly due to both processes' reliance on integrating information across numerous timescales (see \citealp{HassEtal15} for review).  Building from this trend, it reasons that the initial compression of real-world experience into memory and subsequent iterative compressions post-encoding are in fact analogous processes, driven by same neural mechanisms.  vmPFC activity has been linked to a number of tasks necessary to this compression algorithm's function, including evaluating memories' relevance \citep{NieuTaka11}, inhibiting irrelevant memories (along with ventrolateral prefrontal cortex; \citealp{KuhlEtal07, WimbEtal08, WimbEtal09, WimbEtal11, WimbEtal15}), distinguishing between temporally recent and remote memories \citep{BonnEtal12}, compressing representations of time (\citealp{TigaEtal16}; see \citealp{Howa18} for review), stabilizing and reorganizing retrieval networks \citep{TakaEtal07}, integrating featurally similar memories \citep{TseEtal07,ZeitPres10,TseEtal11}, and mediating extinction memory \citep{MadsEtal17}.  Interestingly, vmPFC activity during recall has also been shown to vary with the temporal remoteness of memories in a fashion that is currently ill-understood \citep{MaguEtal01, NikiLuo02, OddoEtal10, BarrEtal18}, underscoring the possibility that a second variable (such as assessed relevance) conflates with time in determining a memory's sampling rate, and thus its situation models' distinctiveness for their corresponding real-world events.  I hope to expand upon this theory in future work that examines delayed recall effects across longer timescales and more follow-up sessions, in order to track the behavioral and neural changes in episodic memory across time and contexts.

The wordles in Figures \ref{fig:a1content}, \ref{fig:delcontent}, \ref{fig:a2content}, and \ref{fig:adcontent} offer an intuitive, content-driven method of visually assessing the most and least memorable features from each episode.  Together, they suggest a delineation between memorability trends in two situations: familiar and unfamiliar contexts.  All participants who viewed the first \textit{Atlanta} episode in session one and those who viewed \textit{Arrested Development} in session two were exposed to an entirely novel set of characters and themes (no participants in this study were previously familiar with either show).  The best-remembered content in such situations (both event-wise and overall) reflected the most significant characters and most salient interactions.  This idea is in keeping with classic findings regarding the adverse effect of information overload on memory ability (particularly memory for persons and characters; \citealp{BargThei85}), as well past results using this same visualization technique \citep{HeusEtal18c}.  When the principal characters were no longer novel, memorability instead appeared to trend with events' uniqueness (scenes involving Alfred, a.k.a. ``Paper Boy" were more thematically variable and remembered better; those involving Earn all occured in a single location and were remembered less successfully).  However, there is a second potential explanation for the memorability patterns in \textit{Atlanta} episode two's content that is difficult to parse out. 

Over the delay period, participants forgot more information about \textit{Atlanta} episode one events they remembered best immediately after viewing than those that were initially difficult to remember.  \cite{BaysEtal11} found that memory resources can be reallocated at will to prioritize goal-oriented information, and that this effect improved recall performance even if both the prioritized and non-prioritized information had previously been encoded. It is possible that by recognizing their own event-varying success during the immediate recall session, participants reallocated cognitive resources toward improving their recall of poorly remembered events (i.e., those involving Alfred's character) both in the delayed recall session and when the character reappeared in the second \textit{Atlanta} episode.  
 
Although they are heavily interpretable, these wordles afford multiple possible explanations for trends in memorability arising from cognitive processes. The results of the exploratory analyses conducted in \hyperref[sec:brainresults]{\textit{Neural correlates of content memorability}} offer a potential complimentary driver of memorability, instead based solely upon physiology.  The present findings suggest that where in the brain certain content is represented may bias the precision with which it can later be remembered.  On average, a greater degree of overlap between events' neural content representations and the default mode network led to those events being remembered better, while the opposite was true of overlap with memory-related regions.  One explanation for this phenomenon comes from the very fact that the semantic system is distributed across numerous other functional regions and networks.  Prior research has found that various areas can, in some cases, contribute to multiple functions simultaneously \citep{SigmDeha08, CharKoec10, RishEtal13}.  However, numerous studies have shown task-induced deactivation (TID) in default mode regions (including precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex) while participants engage in effortful tasks \citep[e.g.,][]{BindEtal99, RaicEtal01, Raic06, McKiEtal06, WeisEtal06, BindEtal09, NakaEtal13}, suggesting that their resting state functions are to some degree interrupted.  This assertion is supported by observed decreases in behaviors related to resting state function during measured TID in default mode areas \citep{TeasEtal95, McKiEtal03, McKiEtal06}.  A decrease in resources allotted by DMN areas to resting state functionality during episode perception would serve to disencumber these regions, allowing them to instead devote greater resources to their roles in the semantic system.  This may in turn lead to more detailed content representations in now-unburdened domains.  Consequently, activity in DMN areas that decreases during effortful tasks is reinstated when the task engages the semantic system \citep{BaumEtal02, HensEtal02, MechEtal03, RissEtal03, IschEtal04, BindEtal05, XiaoEtal05, HumpEtal07}.  

While default mode regions' specific deactivation during narrative perception and recall allows them to better represent overlapping semantic content, memory areas' specific activation in these instances has the opposite effect.  Imaging studies have established a pattern of increased activity in a memory consolidation circuit that immediately follows encoding \citep{TambEtal10, TambDava13, TompDava17}.  This network comprises a broad array of cortical regions, general consisting of the hippocampus, various MTL structures, ACC and mPFC (as well as ventral tegmental area, depending on the task; \citealp{NazaReza13, TompEtal15, KitaEtal17, CastEtal19}).  The potential for this network's encoding and consolidation activity to negatively influence recall precision may be reasoned from modern behavioral findings on immediate forgetting.  \cite{OberLewa08} extensively compared three classes of models describing immediate serial recall dynamics (time-based decay, decreasing temporal distinctiveness, and interference) and found that an interference-based model best fit results from two experiments with varying delay periods.  Critically, they concluded that that obligatory exposure to additional information between encoding and retrieval of a target memory creates concurrent processing demands that interfere with recall ability.  Extending this idea to a naturalistic setting, the processing demands of encoding and beginning to consolidate each subsequent episode event would interfere with the representational clarity of preceding events, particularly in regions where this demand is greatest (i.e., those involved in encoding and consolidation).  Supporting the interpretation of these behavioral and neuroscientific findings as analogues, \cite{OberLewa08} found delay length to have little to no effect on recall ability compared to interference, while \cite{TompEtal15} observed that consolidation network activity began to benefit memory performance a day after encoding.  The resulting loss of interference-based impairment is reflected in my findings, as content overlap with memory areas no longer negatively impacted recall precision after the week-long delay.  Inherent differences in event memorability arising from innate neurophysiological artifacts have enormous potential to influence how we understand humans' capacities to learn and remember, as well as the inaccuracies in in our mental representations of the external world.  The present interpretation constitutes one potential explanation for the network overlap–precision interactions observed here, and these relationships certainly justify further exploration.  


\section*{Concluding remarks}

My data-driven approach to studying episodic experiences and memories offers insights into how and why memories change across time and contexts.  A better understanding of these internal changes lies at the root of alleviating the memory loss that comes with age, creating improved models of teaching and learning, and treating memory disorders of both physical and psychological origins.  I present a novel method of predicting neural activity from behavioral data, the further development of which may help researchers more efficiently focus imaging studies before investing substantial time and money.  Finally, I submit predictions that may be leveraged to drive extensive future research into their implications.  The computational techniques employed here serve to mitigate ever-present opportunities in research for bias and obfuscation, from removing the need for human-generated memory assessments, to drawing upon open-source software tools at each step of the process, to creating and freely sharing reproducible analyses and visualizations, to standing on the shoulders of giants by comparing activation maps to decades of prior research.  Through future work, these findings and methodological advances may serve to improve our knowledge of the world, as memory is the only means by which we can comprehend it.
